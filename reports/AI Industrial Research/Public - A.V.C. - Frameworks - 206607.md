# Understanding the AI Value Chain in One Chart: The Five-Layer Cake, and Where the Money Flows

This year at Davos, Jensen Huang sketched a five-layer cake while talking with BlackRock's CEO.

His point was that AI is not a product; it is an entire industrial system stretching from power plants to smartphone screens. Every layer has its own business, and every layer has its own bottlenecks.

What makes this framework valuable is that it answers a very practical question: in 2026, the four major cloud providers will spend more than $630.0bn on AI. Where exactly will that money go?

Follow the five layers, and the flow of capital becomes clear.

---

## What the Five Layers Look Like

```
Layer 5  Application layer    What you can see: AI assistants, autonomous driving, robots
Layer 4  Model layer          What you can use: large models, multimodal AI
Layer 3  Infrastructure layer What you cannot see: data centers, networking, liquid cooling
Layer 2  Chip layer           What everyone is fighting for: GPUs, memory, packaging
Layer 1  Energy layer         What everyone forgets: power, transformers, nuclear power
```

One line from Jensen Huang stuck with me: "In the future, every company will have two factories - one that produces products, and one that is an AI factory. The AI factory does only one thing every day: produce tokens."

His metric for measuring AI factory efficiency is tokens produced per watt.

In other words, the AI business will ultimately look like manufacturing: it will compete on how much intelligence each unit of electricity can produce. That is why the base of the cake is not chips, but energy. Without electricity, a GPU is just a hot piece of metal.

---

## Layer 1: Energy - Least Sexy, Highest Certainty

According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), global data centers consumed about 485TWh of electricity in 2025 and are expected to double to roughly 950TWh by 2030. Gartner's estimate is slightly lower (448TWh), but the trend is the same: a doubling in five years.

The problem is that there is not enough power. Nearly half of U.S. data center projects originally scheduled to come online in 2026 have been delayed or canceled. The reason is not chips; it is power and transformers.

Average lead times for large power transformers have already exceeded two and a half years, and some high-spec models are backed up for more than four years. That means a transformer ordered today may not arrive until 2029 at the earliest. There is no realistic chance this bottleneck eases before 2028.

The investment logic in the energy layer is simple: no matter which AI model wins, data centers need electricity. You do not need to pick the winning model company; you only need to know that power-infrastructure supply is not keeping up with demand.

Metrics to watch: changes in transformer lead times, the data center power-capacity gap, and nuclear project approval progress.

---

## Layer 2: Chips - The Most Value-Dense Layer

AI accelerator chips are the most capital-intensive part of the whole value chain. Depending on the data source, this market is around $150.0bn in 2026.

Several key dynamics matter:

General-purpose GPUs remain overwhelmingly dominant, with roughly 86% share of the data center AI chip market in 2025. But that number is moving down. Custom chips (ASICs) are expected to grow shipments by 45% in 2026, surpassing the 16% growth rate of general-purpose GPUs for the first time. Large customers are looking for alternatives, and that is a structural change.

HBM (high-bandwidth memory) is an indispensable component of AI chips. More than 60% of global share is concentrated in one company, and supply-demand tightness is expected to last at least until 2028.

For the most advanced logic-chip processes (3nm and below), more than 90% of manufacturing capacity is concentrated at TSMC. Advanced packaging capacity, such as CoWoS, is also tight and has become a new bottleneck.

The chip layer has extremely high value density, but valuation dispersion is also the widest. Some leaders have $5.0tn market caps but only trade at forward P/E multiples in the twenties, while some companies with market caps of only a few tens of billions trade at more than 100x earnings. Pricing differences within the same layer are large.

Metrics to watch: the HBM supply-demand gap, utilization rates for advanced-packaging capacity, and the rate at which custom chips replace general-purpose GPUs.

## Layer 3: Infrastructure - Where the Real Money Goes

This layer is the most intuitive because it has one hard number: the 2026 capex of the four major cloud providers.

| Company | 2026 Capex Guidance | YoY Growth |
|------|------------------|---------|
| Amazon | About $200.0bn | +52% |
| Microsoft | About $190.0bn | +61% |
| Google | $180.0-190.0bn | +97% to +108% |
| Meta | $125.0-145.0bn | +73% to +101% |
| **Total** | **About $630.0-725.0bn** | **About +77%** |

Note: All figures are in U.S. dollars. YoY growth is calculated based on actual 2025 capex. Company guidance is given as ranges; the table uses midpoints or firm values.

Not one of them is cutting. All are raising guidance.

The rough flow of this money: a little over 60% goes to short-life assets such as chips and servers (flowing up into the chip layer), while a little over 30% goes to long-life assets such as data center buildings, land, and network equipment.

The infrastructure-layer investment approach is direct: follow the capex. Six to seven hundred billion dollars will be spent; the only question is whose pocket it lands in. This layer also has an interesting pattern: two companies doing similar work can often trade at valuations that differ by roughly 2x. That kind of peer-pricing gap is one of the easier opportunities to understand.

Metrics to watch: quarterly capex guidance from the four major providers (raised or cut), optical-module and switch backlogs, and liquid-cooling equipment penetration.

## Layer 4: Models - Fastest Growth and Highest Cash Burn

Something extraordinary is happening in this layer: one AI company's annualized revenue (ARR) grew from $1.0bn to $47.0bn in roughly 17 months. To be clear, ARR is a forward-looking metric that annualizes current monthly revenue; it is not the same as cash already collected. But the growth rate is genuinely unprecedented.

The second half of 2026 will be a major listing window for AI companies. Several leading names are preparing IPOs, and public markets will test these sky-high valuations for the first time.

The model layer has several characteristics: growth is astonishing, but valuations are even more astonishing - revenue is doubling while valuations are often 40-50x ARR; the winner structure is not settled, as the top three or four models are rapidly converging in capability; Chinese players are an important variable, with API pricing only a few percent of overseas peers and still falling recently.

For investors, the difficult part of the model layer is that model companies themselves are too expensive. Two adjacent angles may be more worth watching: companies that own unique training data, because models need data and good data is non-renewable; and enterprise-service companies that provide private deployments, because not every enterprise is comfortable handing its data to large-model vendors.

Metrics to watch: ARR growth and retention, API pricing trends, and post-IPO market performance.

---

## Layer 5: Applications - Easiest to Understand, Hardest to Monetize

All the AI that people can feel directly - assistants, customer service, autonomous driving, robots - sits in this layer.

The reality is that most public companies labeled as "AI concepts" still get very little actual revenue contribution from AI. The companies truly earning large amounts from AI are still few enough to count.

Valuation dispersion is most extreme in this layer. At one end are "pure AI companies" with rapid growth but valuations of 100-200x earnings. At the other end are traditional software giants transforming with AI, whose new AI products may be growing at 200-300 percent, but because total company revenue growth is only around 10%, the market still prices them as "old companies."

There is an interesting mismatch here: the market sees 10% total growth, but it does not see AI sub-businesses growing two to three times. If AI revenue share rises from 3% to 15%, total growth could suddenly reaccelerate, and the valuation logic would be rewritten. Of course, that still needs time to be proven.

Metrics to watch: the actual revenue contribution of AI features (not just ARR or users; look for real cash), actual customer usage, and AI's impact on overall gross margin.

## One Pattern After Looking Across the Five Layers

The lower the layer, the higher the certainty, and valuations are usually more reasonable. The higher the layer, the larger the imagination space, but the stronger the uncertainty.

| Layer | Certainty | Upside | Valuation Characteristics |
|------|--------|--------|---------|
| Energy | Extremely high | Low | Often underpriced |
| Chips | High | Medium-high | Leaders are reasonable; smaller companies are overlooked |
| Infrastructure | High | Medium | Large peer valuation gaps |
| Models | Medium | Extremely high | Generally expensive |
| Applications | Low | Extremely high | Severely dispersed |

If you want certainty, start from the bottom and work upward. If you want explosiveness, start from the top and dig downward.

## A Few Investing Common-Sense Rules

First, popular does not equal expensive, and obscure does not equal cheap. Nvidia is the hottest company in the field, but its forward valuation is only in the twenties and growth is above 70% - on that basis, it is not particularly expensive. The key is not whether a company is popular, but the relationship between growth and valuation.

Second, when two companies in the same lane do similar things but trade at valuation multiples that differ by roughly 2x, the market usually corrects that gap sooner or later.

Third, rather than asking "who is strongest," the more useful question is "who controls the bottleneck?" The strongest companies are the most expensive; the bottleneck companies are often ignored.

Fourth, AI is a decade-scale industrial transformation. There is no need to go all in at once. Good companies always have moments when they are not expensive.
