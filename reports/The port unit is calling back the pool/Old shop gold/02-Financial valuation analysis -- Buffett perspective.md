More than 60% of ROE is extremely rare in global consumer goods, after a few top luxury companies. But it needs to be dismantled using the Dubon analysis framework:

# III. Cash flow and balance sheet quality

# 1. Operating cash flow: the biggest red flag

In the first half of 2025, the old shop had net profits of $2,268 million, but ** net operating cash flows had been released of $2,215 million. While detailed cash flow statements had not been disclosed throughout the year, operating cash flows were almost impossible to correct in terms of an increase of $12 billion in inventory and an increase of $4.9 billion in borrowing.

** This means that "profit" on the profit sheet did not translate into real cash inflows, but into gold in the warehouse.**

#2. Inventory: assets or risks?

16 billion yuan in inventory, which represents a very high proportion of total assets. From two perspectives:

** An optimistic perspective**: Gold is different from ordinary consumer stock -- it has intrinsic value, it does not pass seasons, and it does not expire. Stock even adds value at the price of gold. The logic of management's stocking is "socking low-priced raw materials".

** Prudent perspective**: Stock turnover days increased from 195 days to 216 days, indicating that sales were growing faster than they were fully up to the ready speed. If gold prices fell by 10%, 16 billion stock stocks faced potential impairments of $1.6 billion, equivalent to one third of net profit in 2025. The risk is completely naked.

3. Debt structure

Of the $6,264 million in bank loans, more than $6 billion will expire in 2026. This raises two questions:
- **Renewal risk**: Extension may be hampered if the bank tightens its credit terms due to large fluctuations in gold prices
- ** Interest cost**: 4.5 times the size of borrowing and increasing erosion of profits from financial costs

Management chooses not to rush + to borrow heavily, essentially ** to leverage over-gold**. This is not a conservative financial strategy.

# Core conclusion

The financial data on old-leaf gold presents an "extreme contrast": profit statements are impeccable (ROE 65 per cent, Māori 41 per cent, growth 200 per cent +), but cash flow statements and balance sheets warn (business cash flows are negative, inventories 16 billion, borrowing 6.2 billion).

The contrast is rooted in the company's "zero-squid plus mass hoarding" strategy. The strategy is a profit amplifier (low-priced, high-priced) at the higher price, but a risk amplifier at the lower price.

But the real problem is not "is the current PE low enough," but "what is the company’s normal profits after three years." If gold prices return to average, and the rate of increase slows to 20-30%, does the price still provide enough security margins?
