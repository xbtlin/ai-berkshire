Several key observations:

** Income growth is slowing**. From 13.3 per cent in 2022/2023 to 4.8 per cent in 2024/2025, the last half-year has even seen a decline of 1.8 per cent. Growth slowed largely because of rent pressure on Hong Kong and the hinterland, with the overseas portfolio (+4.7 per cent) not fully offset.

The RIT rate (net/total income) is maintained at a long-term rate of 74-75%, with minimal fluctuations. This indicates a highly manageable cost structure - operating costs such as property administration fees, pay differentials, maintenance, etc. are relatively fixed.

** The increase in revenue did not necessarily increase**. Income grew by 4.8 per cent in fiscal year 2024/2025, but net real estate income increased slightly less. The decline in income from 2025/2026H1 was 1.8 per cent, and net real estate income declined by 3.4 per cent, indicating that costs were more rigid than profit declines when income shrunk.

# III. Capitalization capacity

##1. Asset Upgrade (AEI)

The core capital allocation capacity of the organization is reflected in the asset enhancement project.

- 103 projects completed cumulatively
- Average return on investment of 18 per cent
- Individual projects vary in size from tens of millions to hundreds of millions of HK$.

The average return of 18% is quite impressive in the real estate sector. This suggests that the lead shows the ability to identify and implement high-return renovation projects and to transform old malls into more productive modern retail spaces.

#2. Overseas acquisitions

The recent overseas expansion of the leadership includes:

- **Australia**: Large shopping malls such as Sunshine Plaza, Macarthur Square, Lakeside Joondalup
- **Singapore**: Jurong Point, Swing By@Thomson Plaza
- **United Kingdom**: canary pier The Cabot et al.

Overseas property currently contributes about 11.8% of the portfolio value, operating better than Hong Kong’s headquarters (Australian lease renewal + 16.3%, Singapore lease renewal + 12.9%). But overseas expansion also brings about management complexity and exchange rate risks.

#3 Asset disposal and recycling

The lead-paints continuously sell inefficient assets and buy high-efficiency assets. This is a standard operation for RIT to upgrade the quality of the portfolio, but the quality of implementation depends on the timing and pricing of the purchase and sale.

## 4. LREP Private Fund

Launching a third-party capital management operation (LREP) to move from a purely holding REIT to a platform-based management. The long-term potential of this business is a matter of concern, but it is still small in size and has limited financial contributions.

# Capital allocation rating: <unk>

The return on asset enhancement was excellent, and the expansion abroad had begun to bear fruit. But LREP was still in its early stages, and the overall capital allocation capacity needed to be validated in the hands of new management.

# V. Key financial risks

# 1. NAV continues down

The NAV may further decline if the capitalization rate continues to expand (interest rates are higher than expected and property markets are falling). The Pilgrim Project predicts a moderate loss of 2-4% per year in the portfolio valuation for fiscal year 2025-2027.

#2. DPU stagnating growth

Hong Kong and the Mainland are under pressure for rent, and if overseas portfolio growth is not sufficient, the DPU may have levelled off or even slightly declined over the next 1-2 years. This is a risk for investors that rely on distributional growth.

#3 Exchange rate risk

Overseas assets are valued in Australian dollars, Singapore dollars, pounds sterling, and income and valuation are subject to exchange rate fluctuations. Exchange rate risk will become more pronounced as the overseas share rises.

## 4. Increase and dilution of units

The lead agency used to use the APDF unit as a countervalue to dilute each unit of NAV and DPU. If large-scale increases continue in the future, the interests of the current holders will be diluted.
