# I. The Chinese innovation industry

#1.1 Structural changes being experienced by industry

China’s innovation medicine industry is at a historic turning point. Several forces are also reshaping the industry:

** Reconstructed generic pharmaceutical patterns.** Since the "4+7" harvest in 2018, generic prices have been significantly reduced, and profit margins have been sharply narrowed. This has forced almost all capable drug producers to accelerate the transition to innovative drugs. The process of integration is essentially a process of "deportation of cheap currency" – the elimination of companies that simply consume old drugs and the forced upgrading of their industries.

** Innovative drugs commercialized well blowout.** In 2025, five companies with annual revenues of more than $10 billion in innovation medicine in China emerged (Hing Rehung, Bixian, Hansen, Sindha, etc.), and the entire industry has seen revenues from the commercialization of innovative drugs break out by $100 billion. China is making a transition from "the big drug-like" to "the powerful country of innovation medicine."

** The sea is the second growth curve.** License-out deals are innovating in volume and amount, and Chinese drug innovators are increasingly recognized by the international drug industry. Hanson’s collaboration with GSK, Regeneron, Mercado, and Rhodes is a microcosm of this trend.

** But industry is also facing internal info.** Targeting stubbles (the hottest track, such as PD-1, ADC, and GLP-1) have led to increased homogenization competition. Not all innovation drug companies can run out, and industry shuffling is accelerating.

##1.2 Economic characteristics of the industry

The innovative pharmaceutical industry has several unique economic characteristics:

- ** Winner-all-eat effect:** First-in-class or Best-in-class products tend to account for most of the market share in the same target/adaptation disorder
- **The scale effect is not clear:** The success rate of R & D will not increase significantly due to the size of the company
- **Temporal barriers are extremely high:** Even with the funds, it would take more than 10 years to build a competitive and innovative line of medicine from zero.
- ** Policy implications:** Health insurance negotiations, approval speed, collection policies, etc., will significantly affect the profitability of enterprises

# 2.3 Hanson's competitive disadvantage

The Amedaloni has just been approved by the UK MHRA, and collaboration with Glenmark has also covered emerging markets. Hanson has chosen to use the License-out model of "lending ships out of the sea" instead of building a global commercial team.

** Diversity challenges beyond neoplasms.** Hanson, 66.4% of income comes from the anti-cancer area. While the GLP-1 pipe (the metabolic area) and the self-exempt line are advancing, it will take time to achieve real diversification of income.

** Research and development power ranking.** In China's Top 50 ranking in 2025, Hansen's rankings rose significantly (by 17 places), but still lags behind top companies like Henry and the State of Pixia. This suggests that Hansen's innovation, "gold content," is increasing, but there is still a gap between the top and the top of the industry.

# Three, multi-thinking model analysis

#3.1 Evolutionary Perspective: Survival of the fittest

Hanson’s "simulation" transition is a microcosm of Darwinian evolution in Chinese pharmaceutical industry. The collection is like a "natural choice" that eliminates counterfeit pharmaceutical companies that cannot adapt to environmental change. The key to Hansen’s survival and growth is two things: (1) that transformation decisions are decisive enough – to start to innovate when imitating drugs is profitable; and (2) that there is enough "energy reserves" (cash flow and channel resources) to survive the transition.

#3.2 Probability perspective: expectations of the pipe

Innovative drug development is a game of probability. Individual projects may have a success rate of only 10-20%, but Hanson has advanced 40+ projects, 70+ clinical trials at the same time, reducing the risk of failure of the whole combination through the "big rule."

More importantly, Hansen’s strategy through License-out is essentially to "sell some of the space to lock in certain income." First payment and early milestone payments are higher-level income, shifting uncertainty from global commercialization to partners like GSK and Regeneron.

#3.3 Physics perspective: dynamics and inertia

Hanson's current business has a clear "flying wheel" effect:

Innovative drug use  Increased cash flow  Increased investment in research and development  More pipe push/License-out  More revenue  more cash flow...

Once the wheel is turned, it is highly inert. But what needs to be warned is that if Amétini is significantly slowing down as competition increases, the engine of the wheel may be damaged.

#3.4 Psychological Perspective: Narrative Misperity

Market narratives of Hansen are changing from "a company that is hit by a pseudo-pharmaceutical." This narrative shift is usually accompanied by a revaluation. But investors need to be wary of over-inflicted narratives of "successful transformation" without ignoring real risks – such as increased competition for innovation medicines, failure of pipelines, policy changes, etc.
