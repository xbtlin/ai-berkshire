# Analysis of business model of CLP Holdings (0002.HK)

> Analysis dimensions: business essence, moat, user value, corporate culture
> Core question: What is the nature of this business? Can you continue to make money?

---

## 1. The essence of this business

The essence of CLP Holdings’ business can be summarized in one sentence: **With deterministic capital investment in exchange for deterministic returns within a regulated framework**.

Hong Kong’s electricity business is CLP’s core “cash cow”. According to the Scheme of Control Agreement signed with the Hong Kong government, CLP can obtain an allowed rate of return of 8% on average net value for the fixed assets invested in its power-related business. This means: How much money you invest in building power plants and laying out power grids will give you profits based on this proportion. The agreement is valid for 15 years from 2018 to 2033.

The essence of this business model is not "selling electricity to earn the price difference", but "capital investment in exchange for returns". It is crucial to understand this:

1. **Extremely high income certainty**: Electricity is one of the most rigid needs, and the electricity demand of Hong Kong’s 2.83 million customers is hardly affected by the economic cycle. Although Hong Kong's electricity sales will decline slightly in 2025, the fluctuations will be minimal.
2. **Profit anchoring mechanism**: The 8% allowed rate of return is equivalent to setting an "anchor" for profits. Even if operating efficiency fluctuates, the core profit range is guaranteed.
3. **Clear growth logic**: If you want to grow, increase investment in fixed assets. Development plan approvals every five years determine the pace of capital spending.

From the nature of the business, this is more like a "bond-like": stable and predictable, but with limited growth.

## 2. Moat analysis

### 2.1 Natural monopoly barriers ★★★★★

There are only two players in the Hong Kong electricity market: CLP and HK Electric. CLP is responsible for Kowloon, the New Territories and most outlying islands, and HK Electric is responsible for Hong Kong Island and Hok Tsui. There is no competition between the two - each is a complete monopoly within its own power supply area.

This monopoly is not established by technology or brand, but by a combination of physical infrastructure and government concessions. To re-lay a complete network from power generation, transmission to distribution, the investment is hundreds of billions of Hong Kong dollars, and under the current system there is no room for third parties to enter.

This is one of the strongest moats.

### 2.2 Regulatory Framework Protection ★★★★☆

The 8% allowed rate of return is both a protection and a ceiling. It is said to be protection because of this mechanism:
- Passing on fuel cost fluctuations to consumers through fuel fee adjustment provisions
- Ensure there is a bottom line for profits even if the economy goes down
- 15-year agreement period provides long-term certainty

It is said to be a ceiling because:
- The rate of return dropped from 9.99% in 2008 to 8% in 2018
- There is a possibility of further reductions after the agreement expires in 2033
- Excessive investment in fixed assets will be subject to government approval

### 2.3 Scale and technical barriers ★★★★☆

CLP operates a complete integrated power generation, transmission and distribution system in Hong Kong, including large power plants such as Lung Kwu Tan and Castle Peak Power Plant, as well as a transmission and distribution network covering the New Territories and Kowloon. The economies of scale and technology accumulation brought about by this integrated operation cannot be replicated by latecomers in the short term.

99.999% power supply reliability is a moat in itself - meaning outages last for only about 5 minutes throughout the year, among the best in the global power industry.

### 2.4 Brand and user stickiness ★★★☆☆

Frankly, a utility's brand moat is passive. Customers "stick" to CLP not because they like CLP, but because they have no other choice. Although this kind of stickiness is strong, it does not have the ability to achieve brand premium.

**Moat comprehensive rating: ★★★★☆**

The core moat comes from natural monopoly and regulatory protection, which is very strong but lacks active pricing power and growth elasticity.

## 3. User value and differentiation

From the user's perspective, the core value provided by CLP is **reliable power supply**, and this has been done extremely well.

- Power supply reliability of 99.999%, the world's top level
- Covers 2.83 million customers, covering residential, commercial and industrial users
- Although the electricity bill level is not low (the overall price in Hong Kong is relatively high), there is a transparent pricing mechanism under the regulatory framework

In terms of differentiation, CLP has invested heavily in clean energy transformation in recent years:
- Commitment to achieve net-zero emissions by 2050
- Commitment to phase out coal power by 2040
- The carbon emission intensity target in 2030 is 0.26 kg CO2/kWh

However, it must be pointed out that the meaning of "differentiation" in a monopoly market is completely different from that in a competitive market. Users have no alternative, so differentiation is more a response to regulators and public opinion than a competitive strategy.

## 4. Business logic of diversified business

CLP is not only a power company in Hong Kong, it has a presence in four overseas markets:

### 4.1 Mainland China: strategic long-term layout

It has invested in more than 50 power projects in 14 provinces and cities, with an attributable installed capacity of approximately 8,098 MW, of which non-carbon energy accounts for 75%. Operating profit in 2025 will be HK$1.60 billion, a year-on-year decrease of 13.7%, mainly affected by the reduction in Yangjiang Nuclear Power’s on-grid electricity price and limited renewable energy consumption.

**Evaluation**: This is a sector that has strategic value but faces policy and market risks. China's electricity market is moving toward marketization, and intensified competition is a long-term trend.

### 4.2 Australia (EnergyAustralia): The biggest hidden danger

It provides electric and gas services to 2.33 million customers and has an installed capacity of 5,787 MW. However, operating profit plummeted 85.6% to only HK$85 million in 2025, which is almost negligible. Main questions:
- Competition in the retail market is fierce and profit margins are compressed
- Accelerated customer churn
- Rising costs of coal and electricity
- Increase in bad debts

**Evaluation**: This is a perfectly competitive market, which is completely different from Hong Kong’s monopoly model. CLP has no moat in Australia and has not demonstrated a competitive advantage. This is the weakest link in the business model.

### 4.3 India (Apraava Energy): Small and unstable

The equity installed capacity is 1,974 MW, covering wind power, solar power and power transmission businesses. Operating profit in 2025 will be HK$220 million, a year-on-year decrease of 32.8%, affected by the impairment of power transmission assets.

**Evaluation**: The scale is too small, the contribution to the group is limited, and it faces various uncertainties in emerging markets.

### 4.4 Southeast Asia and Taiwan

The volume is smaller, mainly involving some equity projects, and the impact is negligible.

## 5. Corporate culture and long-termism

The Kadoorie family has been running CLP for more than a century, and the family holds about 35% of the shares. This family holding structure has several features worth noting:

**Positives**:
- A century-old family business focused on long-term reputation
- Stable dividend policy - even if profits fall by 10.8% in 2025, the dividend per share will still be increased from HK$3.15 to HK$3.20
-Conservative financial style, with debt ratio controlled within a reasonable range

**Factors to be wary of**:
- The family controls 35%, and there is a risk that the interests of the major shareholders and minority shareholders are not completely consistent.
- Century-old companies may have "organizational inertia" and are slow to adapt to new market environments
- The performance of overseas expansion (particularly Australia) shows that management capabilities may not be replicable across markets

In 2023, Chiang Tung Keung will succeed Richard Lancaster as the group chief executive, becoming the first Chinese helmsman in the history of CLP. This change may bring a strategic perspective closer to the Chinese mainland market, but it will take time to verify.

## 7. Key conclusions

**Do the right thing**:
- Guarding the "gold mine" of Hong Kong, 99.999% reliability proves operational capabilities
- Achieved stable capital returns under the profit control agreement framework
- The dividend policy is prudent and accountable to shareholders

**Questions to be answered**:
- Is the Australian business "temporarily difficult" or a "structural problem"? If it's the latter, should we quit decisively?
- After the expiration of the control agreement in 2033, will the 8% return rate be further reduced?
- The power marketization in mainland China is accelerating. What is the competitiveness of CLP?

One sentence summary: ** CLP Holdings is a utility company with an excellent core business in Hong Kong, but its overseas business diversification has not been effective, which has lowered the overall business quality. Investing in CLP is essentially investing in the stability of Hong Kong’s electricity market. **
