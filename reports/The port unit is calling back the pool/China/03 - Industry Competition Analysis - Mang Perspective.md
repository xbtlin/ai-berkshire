# One, look at coal in reverse: think what's gonna kill it first

The most effective way to analyse an industry is to think about how it dies and then, in turn, to assess the probability and the time frame of these deaths.

# Death One: New energy completely replaces coal power

In 2025, for the first time, China's new energy installed capacity exceeded that of a fire-mounted electric unit, with wind-plus light-voltage machines of about 1.4 billion kW and a fire-mounted electric-powered machine of about 1.35 billion kW. But installed capacity did not equal the amount of electricity generated — the proportion of new energy generated in 2025 was only about 20% due to the intermittent nature of wind-based light, and the electricity still contributed about 60% of the electricity generated.

The storage technology is the key variable for deciding whether new energy sources can completely replace fire power. The cost of storage is currently about 0.3-0.4 yuan/kWh, and the cost of achieving a fully economically viable "new energy plus energy" is lower than 0.1 yuan/kWh. At the current rate of technological progress, this will take about 10-15 years.

** Probability assessment**: The probability that new energy will completely replace the power from the fire by 2035 is < 5%. More likely, the way that the power from the main power source to the power source from the main power source to the power source from the "Stamping Power" and the "Standing Power Power Power" process last 20-30 years.

# Death two: Coal price collapses to industry loss

Since 2016, China’s coal production has declined from about 5.5 billion tons to about 4.7 billion tons, and industry concentration has increased significantly. The JCCP pricing mechanism (the price anchored in the “reasonable range” of $570-770/t) has set the policy bottom for coal prices.

Even if the price of coal falls to $500 per ton (close to the bottom of policy), the cost of self-produced coal in the Xiahua is $179 per ton, leaving over $300 per ton of Maori space. Most small and medium coal companies in the industry cost 350-500 yuan per ton, which will be eliminated first.

** Probability assessment**: The probability of a long-term collapse of coal prices to a loss of < 1%. More likely, coal prices fluctuate between $500 and 700 tons, with industry profits shrinking but not losing overall.

# # Death three: Carbon taxes/carbon emission policies lead to unsustainable operations

China’s carbon market currently covers only the electricity-generating sector, with a carbon price of about 80-100 yuan/tonne CO2. If the carbon market were extended to the entire chain of coal, and the price of carbon rose to more than 200 yuan/tonne, the cost of coal use would be significantly increased.

But in policy reality, China's carbon neutral goal is 2060, with a transition period of 34 years. In the interim, coal’s role will gradually diminish, but not disappear.

** Probability assessment**: Carbon policy has the probability of < 10% unsustainable in the coal industry by 2035.

# The conclusions of reverse thinking

The coal industry does not "smuggling" but "developing." China's coal consumption is expected to peak around 2028, and has slowly declined since then at a rate of 1-2% per year. This means that China's Gods face not a "sustain crisis" but a "growth ceiling".

# Three: The whole industrial chain: who is upstream, who is downstream, who has the right to speak?

##3.1 Upstream: equipment and service providers

The suppliers of coal mining equipment (coal-mining machines, diggers, hydraulic supports, etc.) are mainly the Chinese Coal Industries Group, Trinity Workers, etc. These suppliers have limited bargaining power vis-à-vis such super-customs as the Shunhua.

#3.2 Medium-Swain: Logistics transport

This is the deepest link in the Sant'Assat River. The combination of self-employed railways and ports means:
- Transport costs are 15-20 per cent below market prices
- Transport capacity is not externally constrained
- It can be precisely planned and reduce the backlog of stocks.

Coal companies without their own logistics depend on iron, social transport and public ports, which are more costly and subject to human constraints.

#3.3 Downstream: Electricity, steel, construction, chemicals

The largest downstream powered coal is in the electricity sector (about 60 per cent), followed by steel (about 15 per cent), construction materials (about 10 per cent) and chemicals (about 10 per cent).

The power sector has a strong voice – the combined purchases of the five major power groups (husk, kang, HPR, KG, and China) are enough to influence coal prices. But the JCJ coal regime has locked in price fluctuations, reducing the intensity of the games between buyers and sellers.

** Industry chain voice assessment**: The God is in the upper-middle power of the industry chain. Overpowering upstream suppliers, maintaining stable partnerships with downstream customers through logistics locking and the JCLU mechanism. Not absolute "pricing authority," but strong "cost immunity" and "client lock-in."

# IV. The real impact of new energy alternatives: slower than expected, but more than expected

##4.1 Quantified analysis

In 2025, China generated about 95,000 million kilowatt hours of electricity, of which:
- Fire and electricity: approximately 5.7 trillion kWh (60%)
- Hydropower: approximately 1.3 trillion kWh (14 per cent)
- Wind: about 1.0 trillion kWh (10.5 per cent)
- Photovoltaic: approximately 0.8 trillion kWh (8.5 per cent)
- Nuclear power: approximately 0.5 trillion kWh (5 per cent)
- Other: approximately 2 trillion kWh (2%)

Assuming a 15 per cent increase in the future in the volume of new energy generation (on a recent average) and zero annual growth in the volume of electricity generated by the thermal power generation, by 2030:
- New energy generation: approximately 3.6 trillion kilowatt hours
- Total electricity generation (assuming 3 per cent annual increase): approximately 11 trillion kilowatt hours
- Power generation from fire: about 54,000 million kWh (49 per cent)

By 2035:
- New energy generation: approximately 7.2 trillion kilowatt hours
- Total power generation: approximately 12.8 trillion kilowatt hours
- Power generation from fire: approximately 3.6 trillion kWh (28 per cent)

# 4.2 Impact on the gods

Even if the share of electricity from fire fell to 28% by 2035, the total amount of electricity generated would still be 3.6 trillion kilowatt-hours, corresponding to about 1.5 billion tons of coal demand. With non-electric coal, such as steel, construction materials, and chemicals, total coal demand in China could still be between 3.35 billion tons in 2035 (about 30% lower than the current rate of 4.7 billion tons).

In this "shrunk but not lost" market, the lowest-cost, most-intended firm in the chain is the final winner. Industry shrinks tend to be a good-flung-sale-small and medium-sized coal companies that exit their freed market share and are eaten by the most cost-effective ones.

# VII. Core conclusions

To sum up, the coal industry is like a de-devading lake, with water falling slowly. Fish in the lake are becoming more difficult to survive because of the drop in water levels. But China's god is the largest and deepest dent in the lake – even if the lake falls 50%, the last one is still there.

** An optimistic scenario**: Coal consumption was postponed until 2030, and the gods further encroached on market shares with the restructured size advantage, with ROE remaining above 12 per cent, with a steady increase in the share of the gold.

** Baseline scenario**: Coal consumption peaked in 2028 and dropped by 1-2% in the following year.

** Pessimistic scene**: New energy alternatives accelerate, coal demand drops by 3 to 5% annually, and coal prices fall below $500/tonne. The profit rate has been reduced significantly, but with cost advantage, the dividends rate may fall below 3%.

The most likely path is the baseline scenario. The slow death of the coal industry is a chance for God to "defecate slowly" -- in the middle of the contraction of the industry, as the least costly survivor, enjoying the share of competition that would increase.
