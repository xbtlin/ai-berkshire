# I. THE COMPETITION MARKET MARKET: THE COMMUNITY OF THE COMMUNITY IN HONG

# The structure of the Hong Kong public transport market

Hong Kong has one of the highest public transport use rates worldwide, with more than 90% of daily travel dependent on public transport.

** Market share by mode of transport (approx.):**
- Port iron (light iron): approximately 48-50 per cent
- Bus (Nine, New Bar, etc.): approximately 30-33%
- Bain: approximately 10-12%
- ferry and other: approximately 5-8%

This market structure has been largely stable over the past two decades, and the share of iron and iron in ports is slowly increasing as new lines (such as the South Port Island Line, the Sand Midline, etc.) open.

# Why can no one challenge the Iron?

** Physically non-alternative**: The construction of a railway system requires huge capital investment and several years of construction, and the geographical conditions in Hong Kong (sun, fill Haiti, high-density buildings) make it almost impossible to build a parallel railway system. You cannot dig a new subway line underground in the middle ring to compete with the port.

** At the institutional level**: The iron and steel have statutory monopoly on railway operations, and there is no institutional arrangement to "introduction of a second railway operator". The fact that the government holds 74.4% further ensures this exclusivity – the government is both a regulator and a major shareholder and does not compete with itself.

** Natural monopolies at the economic level**: Railways are typical natural monopolies – high fixed costs, low marginal costs, and only one operator can maximize efficiency. Even in the United Kingdom, where railways are the most marketed, one line is authorized to only one operator.

Conclusion: Hong Kong ' s monopoly status is a result of physical constraints, institutional arrangements and triple guarantees of economic law, which are extremely difficult to challenge.

# Indirect competition: bus and new ways of going out

Although there are no direct competitors, the iron industry faces indirect competition from other modes of transport:

** Bus**: In areas not covered by iron in port (e.g., parts of the New Territories), buses are still the main vehicle. But with new routes such as the North Ring, the iron coverage will be further expanded.

** Nets/Vicca**: The development of Internets in Hong Kong is strictly regulated, with less threat of substitution for iron. However, taxis do divert some of their demand from short trips and late nights.

** Tele-office**: The post-epidemiological trend of tele-officer activity may permanently reduce the flow of passengers during some peak periods. However, data show that passenger traffic in 2025 has returned to above pre-epidemiological levels, suggesting that the impact is manageable.

# II. Analysis of multi-thinking models

# Psychological perspective: cost of sinking and path dependence

The city’s entire city is already planning for the Hong Kong railway network. The price of the dwelling is set at a distance from the railway station (the “railway” premium), business activities are concentrated around the terminal, and the government’s new development zone (the Northern Metropolitan Region) is also structured on railways.

# Ecology perspective: symbiotic relationships

The iron and the city of Hong Kong are not simple service-to-consumer relationships, but rather a symbiotic relationship. Railways shape cities, and urban patterns reinforce their dependence on railways. The malls built on the railway stations are not only the assets of the iron but also the living centres of the community.

# Physics perspective: energy density and efficiency

In high-density cities, railways are the most energy-efficient mode of transport. Every kilometre of passengers is far less energy-consuming, carbon-emitting, and space-occupying than cars. As the global quest for carbon neutrality increases, railways’ comparative advantage will not diminish.

# Economics perspective: Monopolies under price regulation

The fare price for iron is subject to the "plus minus" mechanism: the adjustment range = 0.5 x CPI + 0.5 x movement of the transport wage index - productivity factor. This formula limits the price of iron to a narrower range. The fare price for the iron is even maintained in 2025/2026 (the result +1.45 per cent, below the 1.5 per cent trigger threshold, deferred to the following year).

This price control is a fundamental difference between iron and iron: the iron and steel have a monopoly but not a full pricing power. This limits the profit ceiling for passenger operations, but it also guarantees public affordability and political stability.

# The revelation of the expansion of the port iron overseas

The overseas performance of the Hong Kong Railway provides important evidence against the analysis of competitive advantages:

** UK operations**: Elizabeth Line renewal failed (valued at approximately Pound3 billion), and the Southwest British railway contract probably remains open after it expires. The new British government’s nationalization policy is a direct cause, but it also suggests that the port has no crushing advantage in a pure operating bid that is not run in the R+P model.

**Swedish operation**: The Stockholm commuter railway terminated early, confirming a loss of approximately HK$70 million. The business was in a loss state from the start, suggesting that the operation of the iron industry does not generate profits in all market environments.

** Australian operations**: Melbourne Metro Railways have been extended to 2027, and Sydney subways are in the final stages of preparation.

** Inland operations**: In Beijing, Shenzhen, Hangzhou, the average number of trips per day exceeds 4 million.

** Rethink**: The dilemma of the Hong Kong Iron overseas business suggests that its core competitive advantage is highly dependent on Hong Kong's special institutional arrangements (government grant, R+P model). The pure railways, while excellent, do not have overwhelming barriers to competition in the global market. "Hong Kong Iron" and "Hong Kong Iron" are two completely different companies.

# IV. Long-term trend analysis

# A positive trend

** Urbanization and density rise**: Global urbanization trends are irreversible, and demand for rail transport in high-density cities has increased only to the extent that it has increased.

** Carbon neutral and green travel**: Railways are the most environmentally friendly means of mass transport, and support from governments for railway investment continues to increase. This has facilitated increased policy and financial support for the railway.

** Grand Bay integration**: The number of high-speed passenger traffic in the Guangtao port (over 30 million in 2025) is increasing, and people are moving more and more between the ports.

# Adverse trends

** Hong Kong’s population growth is slowing down**: Hong Kong’s birth rate is continuing to decline, and population growth is largely dependent on immigrants.

** Regularization of remote office**: Although the impact is limited, structural changes in passenger flows during peak periods may permanently reduce the utilization of some routes.

** Structural adjustment of Hong Kong building**: The high interest rate environment, emigration and increased supply may lead to long-term adjustment of Hong Kong building city, with a direct impact on the profits of the iron industry.

** Technological substitution risks (low probability)**: Technologies such as auto-pilot cars, flying taxis, etc., may theoretically change urban traffic patterns, but they are extremely unlikely to replace railways in high-density cities for the foreseeable future (10-20 years).

# Five, negative: What are the circumstances under which the Iron Protector River will be eroded?

** Scenario I: change of rules of the game by the Government**

If the government substantially changes the distribution of benefits in the R+P model – such as significantly increasing land premiums, restricting the right to develop the iron industry, or introducing new regulatory restrictions – the core profit model of the iron industry will be substantially undermined. This risk, though not highly probable (the government is also a major shareholder), is not entirely impossible.

** Scenario II: Decline in the status of Hong Kong cities**

If Hong Kong continues to weaken as an international financial centre, and the exodus of people and economic activity accelerates, the number of passengers, bus station business revenues, and property values will decline in tandem. The value of the iron is essentially tied to the depth of the city’s value.

** Scenario III: large-scale engineering accidents or security crises**

Any major incident could trigger a public confidence crisis and political pressure, leading to a significant increase in operating costs or tightening regulations. The quality dispute over the Shattong project had caused considerable damage to the reputation of the port.

** Scenario IV: uncontrollable capital expenditure**

The HK$140 billion in new railway investments, if they are substantially overspent (reference to the history of the overspending on sand and steel), would seriously erode shareholder returns. The iron industry did record overspending on large projects in the past.

# Overall judgment

Hong Kong has a monopoly of textbooks in Hong Kong, and the moat is extremely wide and deep. But this competitive advantage is highly limited to Hong Kong--the plight of overseas operations has shown that the system has left the R+P model, where the iron is a common railway operator.

Investments in the iron system are essentially an investment in the future of Hong Kong. If you believe in Hong Kong’s long-term value, the iron is one of the best carriers. If you are cautious about Hong Kong’s future, the business model is not unique.

The width of the moat gives it certainty, but the moat limits its growth. This is not a target that doubles you, but a ballast that provides a steady return at a reasonable price.
