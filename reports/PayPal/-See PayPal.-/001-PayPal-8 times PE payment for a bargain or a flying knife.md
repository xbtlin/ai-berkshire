# PayPal: A payment giant with a price-to-earnings ratio of 8 times, is it a bargain or a flying knife?

> Reading time approx. 15 minutes

---

## A set of numbers that will make you re-examine PayPal

Let’s first look at a set of data without making any evaluation:

| Indicators | Data |
|------|------|
| Annual revenue | **$33.2 billion** |
| Annual free cash flow | **$5.6 billion** |
| Net cash | **$15 billion** |
| Active Accounts | **439 Million** |
| Payments processed annually | **$1.79 trillion** |
| Current market capitalization | **36.4 billion USD** |
| P/E ratio | **8.7 times** |
| The stock price is down from its all-time high | **Falled 87%** |

For a company that earns US$5.6 billion in cash annually, the market prices it at US$36.4 billion—equivalent to less than 7 years of cash flow to repay the capital. Its market capitalization is almost equal to one year's revenue.

**If this were a small company that no one had heard of, these numbers would be enough to get any value investor excited. **

But this is PayPal—a company that fell from $310 to $41 and had three CEOs in two and a half years. Cheap is never without reason.

---

## From $310 to $41: What Happened

To understand PayPal today, you must first understand how it fell from the sky to the ground.

**July 2021, stock price $310. ** The epidemic has pushed global consumers online, and PayPal is the biggest beneficiary. The market believes that "e-commerce has changed the world forever" and has given PayPal a price-to-earnings ratio of more than 60 times.

**Then the bubble burst. **

The benefits of the epidemic have faded, and e-commerce growth has returned to normal. What’s even more fatal is that Apple Pay, Stripe, and Shopify have begun to encroach on PayPal’s territory from all directions. PayPal is also chasing cryptocurrencies, buy now, pay later, and super apps—it’s trying to do everything but doing nothing well.

The profit margin dropped from 22.8% to 7.8%. The stock price fell from 310 to 50.

**The bottom will be reached at the end of 2023, and new CEO Alex Chriss will take office to promote the "profit margin priority" reform. ** There are signs of improvement in 2024, with shares rebounding to 80. The market once thought a turnaround was coming.

**In Q4 of 2025, the growth rate of brand checkout will plummet to 1%. The stock price plummeted 10%. **

**In February 2026, the board of directors suddenly changed the CEO and replaced him with a printer seller. ** Shares collapsed again, falling to $41.

This is not a "bottoming out" story. This is the story of "hit bottom → rebound → find that the problem has not been solved → double dip".

##Whose money does PayPal make?

PayPal's revenue comes from four places, and it's important to understand their differences:

### Brand Checkout: The most profitable, but it won’t last long

When you’re shopping online and see the “PayPal button” and click to pay – that’s branded checkout. PayPal takes about 3.5% per transaction, and its profit margin is extremely high.

The problem is: **This button is becoming less and less needed. **

Apple Pay uses facial recognition to complete payment in one second, without jumping to the PayPal page, logging in, or remembering a password. For young people, the PayPal button is an "extra step."

The data already reflects this: brand checkout growth has dropped from 6% to 1-2%. The gap between Apple Pay users (90.5 million) and PayPal users (92.1 million) in the United States is almost zero.

**This is PayPal's core business with the highest profit margin, and it is also the one that has suffered the most pain. **

### Braintree: Fastest growing, but barely profitable

When you pay with a credit card online, you may not even realize that PayPal is processing it behind the scenes - this is Braintree. It is an "invisible backend" that helps merchants process payments.

Braintree is growing rapidly (transaction volume +11%), but in order to grab customers, the price is kept very low. **This is a "lose money and make money" business** - the faster the growth, the lower the overall commission rate.

**The core contradiction is here: the fastest-growing business has the lowest profit, and the highest-profit business cannot grow. **

### Venmo: young people’s wallet

Venmo is the most popular P2P payment application in the United States - splitting accounts between friends, AA payments, and transfers. The monthly active users exceed 100 million, and the brand recognition among young people is extremely high.

The good news is that Venmo is finally making money. Revenue in 2025 is $1.7 billion (+20%), with debit cards growing 60% and merchant payments growing 34%.

The bad news is that $1.7 billion only accounts for 5% of PayPal's total revenue. **Venmo is a “good story” but not a “big business” yet. **

### New business: advertising, stable currency, AI

PayPal is using 25 years of accumulated transaction data to advertise - unlike Meta/Google, PayPal knows what you actually bought, not just what you looked at.

The market value of PYUSD stablecoin soared from 600 million to 4.1 billion, expanding to 70 markets.

AI is used in anti-fraud ($500 million in fraud blocked every quarter) and smart checkout.

**These are attempts in the right direction, but their current contribution to revenue is almost negligible. **

## Moat: Still there, but getting thinner

### Advantages that still exist

1. **The world’s largest online payment network** — a bilateral network of 439 million accounts + 35 million merchants cannot be built overnight
2. **Trusted Brand** — Consumers are more willing to pay when they see PayPal on an unfamiliar website. This trust took 20 years to build
3. **Risk Control Capability** — 500 data points, $500 million quarterly anti-fraud, which has real value to merchants
4. **USD 5.6 billion annual cash flow** — No matter how bad the talk is, this machine is still printing money steadily every year

### The part that is being eroded

1. **Pricing power is declining** - commission rate has been declining year after year (1.73%), and the business mix is increasingly biased towards low profits
2. **Branded Checkout Bypassed** — Apple Pay uses OS-level advantages to make the PayPal button “dispensable”
3. **The user base is shrinking** — active accounts are declining from their peak, and young people no longer regard PayPal as their first choice
4. **Unstable Management** — Three CEOs in two and a half years, uncertain strategic direction

## Cheap or a trap: a key question

The market gives PayPal a price-to-earnings ratio of 8.7 times, which is only one-fifth of the historical median. This means the market is pricing in one of several possibilities:

**Possibility A: The market is wrong and PayPal is seriously undervalued**
- A company with revenue of 33.2 billion and cash flow of 5.6 billion is not worth a market capitalization of only 36.4 billion.
- 6 billion repurchase every year, the outstanding shares will be reduced by 50% after 3 years, and the earnings per share may double
- Venmo and advertising are free options
- Michael Burry opened a position at $49, indicating that smart money sees value

**Possibility B: The market is right and PayPal is slowly declining**
- Brand checkout share loss is structural and irreversible
- Apple Pay will replace most online PayPal checkouts within 5-10 years
- Payment is becoming infrastructure, and no one is willing to pay a premium for infrastructure
- Buybacks cannot outperform the shrinking business

**The only question that determines which side you stand on is: can brand checkout stop falling? **

If it can - 8.7 times PE is a bargain once in 10 years.
If not - low PE is not a safety cushion, but a recession warning.

## Signals worth tracking

If you're considering investing in PayPal, here are a few signals that matter more than any analyst report:

**Bull signal:**
- Brand checkout quarterly growth rate returned to above 3%
- Accelerated merchant adoption of Fastlane one-click checkout
- Venmo’s quarterly revenue growth maintains 20%+
- New CEO presents clear and executable strategy

**Bearish Signal:**
- Brand checkout growth rate drops to zero or negative growth
- Active accounts fell below 350 million
- Operating profit margin continues to decline
- Another CEO change

## The last paragraph

PayPal reminds me of a metaphor.

It's like a leaking dam. The dam itself—$33.2 billion in revenue, $5.6 billion in cash flow, and $15 billion in net cash—remains solid. But the cracks — brand checkout slowdowns, management turmoil, Apple competition — are widening.

The price-earnings ratio of 8.7 times is the repair price given by the market.

If the cracks can be plugged, the price is extremely cheap. If it cannot be blocked, the dam will eventually erode.

Michael Burry's position at $49 shows that he is betting on the former. The market's fall to $41 shows that most people are betting on the latter.

Which side are you betting on?
