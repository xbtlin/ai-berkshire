# How wide is the moat of Uber?

The word "platform company" has been used to rot. The drop is the drop, the DoorDash is the drop, and even the used website Craigslist is the platform -- their moats are so far apart. The drop is winning Uber, Craigslist in China, a vertical opponent, who is holding 67% of the share in the U.S. take-back.** The same thing is called the "platform," and fate can be very different.**

Is there a moat in the real sense of the Uber platform? Is the moat narrow or wide? This one is broken from three layers to one -- some deeper than you think, some lighter than you think.

# First layer of moat: the network effect

# How do you turn the wheels #

Uber's flight-wheel logic is simple:

> More Drivers Online <unk> Passengers are waiting shorter <unk> More Passengers are using <unk> More individual drivers pick up more <unk> More drivers are willing to go online

Once the wheel turns, it becomes extremely difficult to catch up.

- ** United States travel market**: Uber **76%** share, Lyft approximately **24%** (source: Nasdaq/Statista 2025 data)
- Lyft market value approximately **$8 billion**, Uber **1/20**
- The oligarchic pattern has been solid for more than five years, and the third is almost non-existent.

Why Lyft knows that density is important but not keeping up? Because the network effect has a cruel feature:** Once the winner's trend towards eating is formed, the catcher faces not a linear disadvantage, but an exponential disadvantage. When Uber has a driver in a city three times more than Lyft, the passenger waits are not reduced by three times, but by a much shorter (because the marginal effects of geographical density are increasing).

# But the network effect has a fatal weakness

The network effect is ** localized**. Uber's driver density in New York doesn't help Los Angeles directly. Each city is a separate battlefield, and needs to build a density advantage independently.

This means:
- The Uber moat is not a "globally united Great Wall" but ** hundreds of cities have walls.**
- In mature markets (United States, Europe, Australia) walls are high.
- In emerging markets, walls can be broken by local rivals at any time.

** Reverse view** Some would argue that the localization of the network effects means that the global moat of Uber is actually not as deep as it looks. This is a valid idea – Uber has already lost to droplets in China and to Grab in South-East Asia, proving that local competitors can win.

# Second layer of moat: cross platform synergy

This could be the most underestimated moat of Uber.

Why is this important?

**Uber One does what you do to make a "save transaction" into a "suspendable subscription".** When a user pays $9.99 per month, he will use Uber instead of Lyft or DoorDash in an instinctive manner -- because "you have paid for it, don't waste it." This is the **sink cost effect** in behavioral economics.

More crucial is the cross-platform effect:** Users who use both travel and takeout, consume about three times as much as single service users.** Uber One transforms two separate operations (outbound and outlet) into a mutually reinforcing ecology.

This is not replicable by Lyft and DoorDash:
Lyft, just travel, no takeout.
- DoorDash, takeout only, no travel.
- Only Uber can provide a combination of "outbound plus takeout plus membership."

** Counterpart **: Some would say that 50 million members sound a lot, but only about 30% of the penetration rate is on the Uber global user base (about 170 million live monthly), and neither the growth space nor the ceiling are certain. And that members eliminate very low costs – and users may lose quickly once competitors offer better concessions. This concern is justified, and Uber One’s long-term retention rate data are not yet sufficient.

# Out vs takeout: two very different patterns of competition

# Go on: Ruler

Uber's share of the United States market **76%** is a near-monopolistic position.

Why does the travelling market create such a pattern?

1. ** Supply side scale effect**: Division opportunities to select the platform with the highest order volume, forming "the strongest and the strongest"
2. ** Brand penetration**: In many countries, "playing Uber" has become the adage "circle."
3. ** Data flyer**: more travel data * Better pricing algorithms and route planning * More efficient * Less expensive

Uber is globally strong in North America, Europe, Australia, Latin America, and the Middle East (through Careem). The exited markets (China, South-East Asia) are smart stoppages.

Why didn't the takeout market show the winner eats all?

1. ** Consumer multiplatform use**: takeout users often wear DoorDash and Uber Eats, which have a preference
2. ** Multi-platform Multi-Pilot Relocation**: Dining room is usually online on multiple platforms at the same time and is not exclusive
3. **Text cost is extremely low**: from Uber Eats to DoorDash only needs 30 seconds

** That means Uber's moat at the takeout market is much weaker than the travel market.**

But there is no need to be too pessimistic – Uber Eats’ international operations (Canada, Japan, Australia, etc.) are strong, and the cross-platform effect of Uber One provides a different line of defense for the takeout business. DoorDash cannot give users a "dock on DoorDash" car.

# Freight: no food, no shame

Uber Freight (Folder) SY2025 Income ** $5.1 billion**, representing 10 per cent of total income, has just achieved the adjusted EBITDA profit/loss balance.

This is a highly homogenous market, and the technological advantages of Uber have not yet been translated into pricing capabilities in the face of established logistics companies such as C.H. Robinson and XPO.

From an investment perspective, the contribution of freight operations to the Uber valuation is close to zero - if this business is cut off, Uber's share price may not be significantly affected.

** But the other way around: ** The cost of retaining the shipping business is low (no more substantial losses) and it provides the Uber distribution network with additional data and resources. This is more like "retention options" than "core engines".
