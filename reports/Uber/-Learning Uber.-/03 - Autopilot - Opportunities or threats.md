# Two opposite futures

There are two completely opposing narratives in the market regarding the impact of autopilot on Uber.

# EXPECT narration: "Uber becomes auto-driving Andre."

The logic is:

Auto-driving car created, but someone needed to help it find the passengers.
Uber has the largest travel demand network worldwide (**28 million +** trips per day)
3. Most autopilot companies (except Waymo and Tesla) do not have consumer brands and access capabilities
Therefore, autopilots need Uber as a distribution channel - like App developers need Andre/iOS

The fact that supports this narrative:
- Uber has signed a cooperation agreement with **20 autopilots.**
- Plan 2026 to provide autopilot services in **15 cities** by the end of the year
- Q1 2026 Number of autopilot trips increased on a year-on-year basis **10 times** (but absolute numbers remain small)
"The biggest autopilot facilitator in the world."

If this story is true, autopilot is a significant benefit for Uber **: 72% of drivers are not required, platform output can be significantly increased from 28%, and profit rates double or even more.

# Pessimistic narrative: "Uber is bypassed"

The logic is:

1. The strongest autopilots (Waymo, Tesla) have their own taxis, App
They don't need Uber - they have skills, cars, brands, money.
Once the autopilot is widely available, consumers will use Wymo App or Tesla App to call cars.
Uber's role as an intermediary was completely eliminated -- just like the real bookshop was canceled by Amazon.

The fact that supports this narrative:
- Waymo has developed and operated his own cab, App. Users have good experience.
- Tesla clearly doesn't run Robotaxi through Uber.
- Uber recently publicly criticized former partner Waymo, with over $10 billion ** working with Rivian, Lucid, Nuro to build their own auto-pilot fleets — this "fail-up-tough-in" is itself a sign that management is worried about being bypassed.
- CEO Dara said at the financial conference that AV mass penetration might need to take place **10-20** ** ** but Waymo seems to be moving faster than that.

# Calm analysis: probabilities and time lines

Both narratives are supported by facts, but both make a mistake:** a complex, gradual process has been simplified to a non-black or white conclusion.**

The closer judgment is hierarchical:

## Short-term (2026-2028): limited impact

- Autopilot currently operates only in a few cities, specific areas, specific weather conditions
- Waymo travels 450,000 times a week, sounds a lot, but Uber has **28 million times a day ** ** 2.5% of the Uber **
- In the short term, the impact of autopilot on Uber's business is almost negligible.
- Uber's $9.8 billion FCF is completely unaffected at this stage.

# Midway (2028-2032): Merger

- Autopilots may operate on a large scale in 10-20 major cities
- Uber may face two situations at the same time:
- Cooperation with some AV companies (for supply of traffic for vehicle supply)
- Direct competition with Wymo/Texra
- Uber 's 10 billion plus self-built fleet input begins to yield returns (or losses)
- Key variables: **Uber ' s own AV fleet is competitive**

## Long (2032+): re-format

- If autopilot is really mature (costs are lower than human drivers, cover most of the scenes), the industry will be completely re-established
- The pattern of competition will depend on: ** who owns the largest demand network + who owns the lowest-cost fleet**
- Uber has the advantage of a network of needs, but not necessarily of fleet costs
- It's a problem that cannot be prejudged at this time.

#Uber's coping strategy

Uber, the current autopilot strategy can be summarized as ** "Multiple Bets + Self-Constructed Insurance"**:

#1. Work with 20+ AV

Partners include Waymo (relationships are deteriorating), 100 degrees Apollo (Dubai), Wayve (London), Avride (Daras), etc. Logically: eggs are not placed in a basket.

#2 Invest 10 billion + self-built convoys

Working with Rivian, Lucid, Nuro, to purchase/custom autopilots. This is a major strategic shift – Uber sold its autopilot R & D department APG in 2020, and now reinvested another 10 billion in building its own fleet.

** Is this a wise move or a panic move?**

** Supporters say **: If you don't build a convoy, you give it to someone else. Waymo is already developing his own taxi app, and if Uber doesn't have the supply, it's completely passive in the future.

** The opponents say **: $10 billion is invested in an area where neither the technical route nor the business model is certain, but gambling itself. Uber has the central advantage of a light asset platform model, with self-built motorcades "inversely" . And Uber is not good at hardware and autopilot -- it tried to sell it, and it failed.

#3 Investment in recharge infrastructure

Input **100 million** to build fast-load stations dedicated to auto-drive vehicles. This is a relatively small but smart bet.

# 4. CEO public pronouncement

Dara recently publicly criticized Waymo's pricing strategy, while claiming that Uber would remain "indispensible" in the autopilot era. This shift in attitude from "partner" to "competer" suggests that Uber management's awareness of the threat of autopilot is rapidly escalating.

# Key variable: who has the "demand" and who has the "supply"

Understanding the impact of autopilot on Uber, the core frame is ** "Design vs Supply"**:

** Supply end** <unk> <unk> <unk> <unk> <unk> <unk> <unk> <unk> <unk> <unk> (millions of independent drivers) <unk> <unk> <unk> (a few AVs own a convoy)<unk>
<unk> The role of **Uber** <unk> the setup platform (smoking 28%)<unk>

In the human driver era, the supply side was highly fragmented -- no driver had bargaining power, and Uber could be steady at 28 percent.

In the autopilot era, the supply side was highly concentrated — Waymo, Tesla each had tens of thousands or even hundreds of thousands of vehicles, and they had the ability and the power to bypass Uber.

** This is the core structural risk to Uber: supply ends are changed from "dispersed" to "centralized", and the platform's bargaining power is weakened**

Uber's investment in 10 billion self-built convoys is essentially a hedge against this risk -- if it's not a "circumstancer," it's a supply.

# One analogy: help you build your instincts

Imagine the history of the taxi industry:

- ** 1990**: Taxi companies own vehicles and drivers, control supply
- **2010s**: Uber appeared, connecting supply (drivers) with demand (passengers) and killing taxi companies by algorithms
- **2030s**: Autopilots own vehicles and do not need human drivers - what's the role of Uber?

Two possibilities:
Uber became the New Age Taxi Dispatch - Auto-Driving vehicles get orders through Uber
Autopilots do their own dispatching -- Uber became "the taxi company of the last time."

** Both results, so far no one can tell.** The right person is lying.
