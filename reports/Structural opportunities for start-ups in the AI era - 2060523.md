# AI blew up a mouth in the walls of the big factory

One problem I have been thinking about for a long time: in the Internet age, the situation of start-ups is getting harder. From 2015 to 2023, the number of new unicorns in China declined each year, and the same is true in the United States.

But in the age of big models, I observed some anomalies. Midjourney’s team, with about 100 people, earns $500 million a year, generates $4.7 million per person – 2.6 times as much as Google. Cursor’s team, with fewer than 50 people, has been passively playing Microsoft GitHub Copilot on the developers’ tool track. Perplexity is valued at over $10 billion, challenging Google searches positively. DeepSeek has trained a model of GPT-4 with perhaps only one tenth of Google’s resources.

This is not an isolated phenomenon, but a sign of some structural change.

I'm trying to figure out: What is the bottom logic of this change? Will it last? Is it really not the power of big companies?

# Internet age: why small companies are getting harder

Understanding the opportunities of the AI era requires understanding why small companies in the Internet age are not.

** Core reason is that "production materials" are monopolized by large companies.**

The business model of the Internet platform is essentially a flying wheel: the platform captures user-to-user-to-user-to-user-to-to-do data-to-data-research/advertise model-to-model models that enhance user experience and advertising efficiency and attract more users. The faster the wheel turns, the harder it becomes to access.

For example, Google handles 8.5 billion search queries every day. Every search, every click, every "user has a third result, not the first" is training data. An entrepreneur can do better than that, without it, the quality of the search is not going to go up. Microsoft must invest billions of dollars, and the market share is always hovering in places.

The same goes for electric power providers. The billions of commodity data, users' views, and purchases of behavioral data make their referral systems more accurate. A new power company platform, no data for the cold start-up phase, no recommended results, no user experience, less data -- a negative spiral.

Social networking is more effective. Your friends are all on micro-mails, and you can't move to another communication tool. It's not about how good the product is, it's about the cost of moving.

So the "barrel formula" of the Internet age can be summarized as: ** Big users, big private data, better models, better experiences, more users. Once built, the cycle is almost unbreakable.

Small companies are naturally at a disadvantage in this game, and unless you can find a brand new class (like a tremor that finds short videos), you can compete positively in the existing category, with a very low success rate.

# Big models change what

And when the big model comes along, every link in the "bar formula" is changing.

# First change: knowledge is "advanced"

In the Internet age, the core barrier is ** private data-driven models. You can't train that model without those data.

An unprecedented step was taken in the pre-training phase of the large model:** almost all the public information on the Internet was incorporated into the model.** Wikipedia, academic papers, code warehouses, news, books, forum discussions — the knowledge of trillion words was compressed into model parameters.

And then, the open source. DeepSeek opened up to V3 and R1, Meta opened to Llama and Ali to Qwen. This means that ** a 10-person entrepreneurial team can get free a model of hundreds of millions of dollars trained to contain most of the open knowledge of humankind**

This is inconceivable in the Internet age. You can't let Google open up the search ranking model to you, and the papi can't share the recommended algorithms with the training data.

But in the age of big models, this is happening.

Does that mean that the data barriers have disappeared completely? No. Large companies still have a large amount of private data — user records, social relations, browsing behaviour. But the key change is:** for most AI applications, the type of data needed has changed**.

Traditional search engines require billions of search logs to optimize their sequencing. But a high-quality code-filled interactive data is needed by an AI programming assistant, and legal case and user-consultation data are needed by an AI legal assistant.

Cursor's programming data, Perplexity's search dialogue data, Midjourney's image generation preferences -- all of which they accumulated in product overlaps. Google has 8.5 billion traditional search queries, but it does not have the dialogue-based search interactive data that Perplexity does.

** Data barriers have not disappeared, but the barrier's "start line" has been replaced.**

# Second change: the exponential decline in the costs of reasoning

Some would say that even if the model were to open up, it would be expensive to run. Can an entrepreneur afford the cost of a large-scale reasoning?

Two years ago, it was a real problem. But the cost of reasoning fell much faster than most people expected.

At the beginning of 2023, the reasoning cost of GPS-4 was about $60 per million output dictionaries. By the end of 2025, DeepSeek V3 provided a similar level of capacity, which fell to less than $1.** For two years, the reasoning cost of equivalent capacity decreased by more than 98 per cent.**

At the hardware level, the efficiency of the reasoning of the GPU of the Great Britain has increased two to three times. At the software level, technologies such as quantification, speculating decoding, model distillation, etc. have continued to reduce costs. The open source reasoning framework (vLM, TensorRT-LLLM, etc.) allows small teams to deploy models efficiently.

Large companies do have a cost advantage in super-larger reasoning – Google has self-researched TPUs and Amazon has Trainium chips. But the key is: ** Most AI applications do not require super-large reasoning. ** A vertical AI product that serves 10,000 corporate clients does not require Google-level infrastructure. The costs of reasoning are low to "no longer be a bottleneck for start-ups."

# Third change: "Dual Multiplier Effect" for small teams

That is the change I think is most underestimated.

AI is not just a "product orientation" for start-ups, but also an "product-product-tool" for start-ups. An AI-born entrepreneurial team can use AI-writing codes, AI-design, AI-data analysis, AI-server, AI-in-content production.**A 20-person start-up companies, with productivity that may be the same as 200-person companies in the traditional era.**

This creates a "double multiplier": AI is both a product you sell to your customers and an internal efficiency-enhancing tool.

The big companies are also using AI tools. But the big companies use AI in a way that is "overlayed" -- they add an AI link to the existing process. The start-up companies use AI in a way that is "original" -- the entire workflow is designed around AI.

The difference is similar: the traditional car company mixes a motor in a fuel truck and Tesla designs pure electricity from the bottom plate. Overlaying is never efficient enough for the original design.

** The organizational coordination costs of large companies cannot be eliminated by AI.** Teams of 100 require management, cross-sectoral coordination, process and approval. These coordination costs do not disappear because each person uses AI. But teams of 20 people do not need these – AI has directly magnified each's output, and there is no loss of friction in the organization.

The case of Midjourney is illustrative: about 100 teams, no external investors, no sales teams, no market sector, with $500 million a year, generate 2.6 times as much per capita income as Google. Even when the team was just over $200 million in 2023, it was only 40 people.

# The most powerful rebuttal and honest response

With these arguments in mind, there are several counter-arguments that I believe are indeed worthy of merit and that must be responded to positively.

Rebut one: Distribution channels are still in the hands of big companies

That is the strongest rebuttal.

Google can put Gemini in search (two billion users a day), Microsoft can plug Copilot in Office (400 million payers), and Apple can plug AI in every iPhone.

That's right. The distribution advantage is real and very strong in the short term.

But history tells us a pattern:** tied distribution can bring in initial users, but it cannot be left behind if the product is not the best.**

Microsoft has IE browsers pre-positioned on every computer, but Chrame won. Microsoft should be integrated in Windows, but Google searches won. Google has Hangouts in Gmail and Android, but WhatsApp won.

Why? Because users choose decisions about products, they often choose between "good enough" and "best". When alternatives are moved at a sufficiently low cost, the advantages of tied distribution are covered by the differences in product quality.

The migration costs of AI products are usually low. Switching from Google to Percy requires only one bookmark. Switching from GitHub Copilot to Cursor requires downloading a new editor. This is not like moving from micro-mail to another chat tool -- you don't need to take your social relationships with you.

** More importantly, start-up companies should not compete in the existing landscape of large companies, but should create new ones.** Cursor does not do a better auto-completing plugin in VS Code -- it redefines the "AI-assisted programming" workflow.

When you create a new class of goods, the distribution advantage of large companies is less efficient, as their distribution channels are designed for older groups.

# Rebut two: The network effect is not gone

Some argue that AI products also have a web effect: more users – more interactive data – models are better able to attract more users. Isn’t that the same as in the Internet age?

The difference is in the intensity and form of network effects** which differ.

The social network effect is "index type" -- the value of the network is squarely proportional to the number of users (the law of Metcalf). You have 10 friends on micromail, and the value of micromail is 100; 100 friends on micromail, the value is 10,000. This network effect is almost unbreakable.

The data network effect of AI products is ** " logarithmic"** - the marginal value of the first 1,000 data is much greater than the marginal value of the 10 million to 10.1 million data. Model training has a diminishing marginal benefit.

This means that it is much less difficult for later trackers to catch up than it is for social networking. You don't need 8.5 billion search data like Google, and you probably need 100 million high-quality data to achieve 90%.

**The network effect of the AI era is weaker, slower and easier to catch up.** This is good news for start-up companies.

# Rebut three: big companies are all involved in AI and they have the talent and the resources to do it

Yes, Google, Microsoft, Meta, byte beats are all in a big bet on AI. They have the world's best research team, almost unlimited budgets, and ready engineering infrastructure.

But here is a structural paradox they cannot avoid ** the plight of innovators.**

Google searches contribute about $175 billion in advertising revenue every year. If Gemini answers directly to the user, the user stops clicking on the search results, what about the advertising income? Google doesn't know AI searches are better, it doesn't allow AI to search too well, because it eats its core business.

Microsoft Office earns more than $50 billion a year from company subscriptions. If Copilot really does get 80% of the Office automatically, does it need so many Office seats? Microsoft faces the paradox that making Copilot too strong might reduce the Office’s needs; and making Copilot not strong enough to lose to start-ups.

It is not about capacity, it is about the structure of the interests. The managers of large companies are not unwise, but their KPI and organizational incentives determine that they must protect stock operations first.** In the "innovation could destabilize their core business" scenario, the rational choices of large companies are conservative.**

And Perplexity doesn't have to protect the revenue from the ad search, and Cursor doesn't have IDE authorization fees to maintain. They can do the best they can, without "self-disruption."

This contradiction was particularly acute in the AI era, as AI threatened not the marginal business of large companies but their core cash bulls.

# Rebut four: Corporate clients trust big brands more

In the corporate market, customers do tend to choose large companies. The phrase "No one gets fired for buying IBM" still makes sense in the AI era. Compliance requirements, service guarantees, brand trust -- these are the advantages of big companies.

But there are two changes here:

First, AI technology is too new, and big companies do not build brand awareness about AI applications. Google's Bard (now Gemini) released it because of factual errors that caused stock prices to drop. Microsoft's Copilot is not well-known in the company.

Second, more and more business clients realize that in the AI area, the best products are often from start-ups rather than big companies. Cursor's reputation in the programmer community is better than GitHub Copilot. Jasper has created a stronger brand in the marketing content generation area than Microsoft.** Trust follows product quality, not company size.**

Of course, in highly regulated industries (medical, financial, government), the ability of large firms to comply is indeed a real advantage. Entrepreneurs need more time to build trust in these areas.

# Special considerations in the Chinese language

The analysis above is based mainly on a global perspective. China has a number of special situations that deserve to be discussed separately.

** The US export restrictions on China’s chips have prevented China’s AI from gaining access to the most advanced GPU. But the constraints have led to efficiency miracles like DeepSeek – using less computing and smarter algorithms to train models comparable to the best levels in the world.

** China’s Internet platform is being relaxed.** The social monopoly of micro-trust is being torn apart by a wave of sound, and the power-market power of treasure-hunting power is being challenged.

** China’s regulatory environment is more restrictive for AI start-ups.** Content review, data compliance, model filing – these compliance costs are not a small burden for small companies. But this also creates a certain threshold of entry, protecting firms that have already done so.

# So what kind of AI start-up company has the greatest chance

So much, not saying, "You can win by doing whatever you want with an AI." On the contrary, start-up companies need to know very well where their structural strengths are, avoid the strengths of large companies and magnify their strengths.

** First, to do the application layer, without making the basic model layer.** Training in basic models does require a huge amount of capital and intensive input of top talent. But the application layer does not. The competitive element of the application layer is a profound understanding of user needs, expertise in the vertical field, and a very rapid product rotation speed -- all of which are the strengths of the start-up companies.

** Second, creating new jobs without optimizing the old ones.** Do not do "better Google Search" and "dialogue information acquisition." Do not do "better code completion" and "better AI original programming environment." Do not do "better PPT tools" and "automated data to visualization to present full links." New jobs do not allow distribution advantages and user inertia to be lost in large companies.

** Third, choice of vertical areas rather than generic platforms.** The universal platform is the home of large companies. But in vertical areas such as law, medicine, architecture, supply chain management, education, the big companies lack expertise in the field and the incentive to go deep into these "small" markets.

** Fourth, arming an AI team and keeping it very lean.** Don't expand it too early. Midjourney's story of about 100 people making $500 million in income and $4.7 million per capita shows that teams in the AI era need not be big and sophisticated. For every more people, one more coordination cost. Use the AI tool to the extreme, making everyone "one man's army."

# An honest conclusion

My core judgement is that: **Inventive companies faced a much better structural environment than in the Internet.** Not because big companies are weaker, but because three bottom conditions have changed:

First, the Open Source Large Model has achieved the equalization of basic capabilities, and the gap between the initial capacity of start-ups and large companies has narrowed considerably.

II. The AI tool has far more empowering effect on small teams than on large ones, as small teams have no loss of tissue friction, creating a "double multiplier effect".

Thirdly, AI threatens the core business of large companies, making innovators more vulnerable than ever. The more successful the big companies are, the more painful they are, the more they are to destabilize themselves.

But I will not say "big companies are doomed to fail." Large companies still have great advantages at the infrastructure level, on universal platforms, in strong regulation of industries, in situations that require mega-distribution.

The real picture is: **AI created a window period, during which small companies had an unprecedented opportunity to build their own moats -- with better products, faster overlaps, deeper field knowledge.** This window will not be open forever. When the industry pattern stabilizes, large companies will return with capital and distribution of advantages.

So, the key for AI entrepreneurs is not "Half or not" -- opportunities are structural, they do exist. The key is ** Speed**: Can we build up what big companies can't buy with money -- a deep understanding of a vertical area, a highly sticky user relationship, a fast-changing product culture during the window period.

These things, they can't be bought, they can't be piled up. Only small and focused teams can do it.
