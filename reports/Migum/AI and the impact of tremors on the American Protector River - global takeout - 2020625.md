I. Impact of AI sales and alcohol travel

# Takeout: AI strengthens the moat, not weakens

The core barriers to take-out are the network effects of the physical world – the density of the merchants, the size of the riders, and the immediate distribution infrastructure. AI is here as an efficiency tool (readiness, route planning, demand forecasting), and the US is the biggest player, because the amount of data and the scene is the most abundant. There are fears that the AI assistant will change the entrance (the user said to AI “show me” instead of opening the US regiment), but ultimately the compliance will go back to the platform of the merchants and riders, and the AI assistant will probably turn into the US Corps’ distribution channel rather than a substitute.

# Liquor Brigade: AI's threat is relatively greater, but limited

The Liquor Brigade is essentially an information-based business, and AI does travel planning and hotel advice, which can theoretically bypass the OTA. But supply-side contract negotiations, price control, stock management, and dirty work, AI cannot solve.

# And conclusions

AI's against the USS is a smooth, not a backwind, and the real variable is how competitors use AI, not AI itself.

# Three, the real threat to the core values of the United States (outwards + distribution networks)

The analysis of "Who's Taking the Legion of America's Business" was done earlier, but the real threat often came not from competitors but from structural changes.

# Threat One: Rideback cost rises (medium term, high certainty)

The distribution network is the moat, but it is the biggest cost. The moat is essentially a few million flexible riders. The problem is:

- China ' s constant promotion of the economic and labour security of casual workers, and the pressure on the regulation of the social security of riders and work injury insurance will only grow.
- Long-term tightening of rider supply with demographic changes (reduced young labour force)
- But consumers have limited resilience to higher prices, especially in the context of declining consumption

The deeper the moat of the distribution network, the greater the cost. This is not the problem with a competitor that can defeat the Corps, but the profit ceiling of the business may be lower than the market expects.

# Threat II: no one to distribute the removal of cyberbarriers (long term, high uncertainty)

The barrier to the distribution network is the "millions of riders plus dispatch algorithms", if no one is to be able to do it to scale:

- **Good news**: Significant decrease in single distribution costs and improved profitability
- ** Bad news**: Barriers change from "human network" to "capital plus technology" and capital and technology can be replicated.

The Corps itself is investing in unmatched supplies, which, if it can lead the way, will strengthen the moat. If this technology is generalized, the barriers to distribution networks are downgraded from the 10-year stack of physical networks to the "rich infrastructure."

The time window for this threat may be 5-10 years, with considerable uncertainty, but in a determined direction.

# Threat Three: Kyoutung Outer (Recently, in certainty)

In 2025, the Japanese government sent a new agency to sell it, and Liu was personally promoting it to better pay the rider. This is the most recently discussed threat in the market. But the assessment is that it is overestimated:

- You've tried to take out hundreds of times, drops, all of them. You can't do it with money and flow.
- The logistics gene of Kyoutung is a warehouse-to-home model, not an instant-to-move mode (store-to-home) and capacity does not migrate directly
- The commercial density required for the take-out is extremely local (3 km per block), and the capital does not accumulate.
- The burner subsidy can bring price sensitive users, but the subsidy stops and the user goes back. - This script has been on many times.

The more likely outcome of the Kyoto-Toto Outer Space is that some shares are available in some cities, but it is impossible to shake the basics of the United States Mission and eventually become a business that is either profitable or losing in the long run.

# The scale gap: The Corps is another measure

The average US-day order is more than 10 times that of DoorDash. The penetration and frequency of the Chinese-foreign market is far greater than in the US – Chinese consumers actually make takeout as a daily and American consumers as an occasional convenience.

# Profitability: The Corps is far ahead

The USS net profit is $4.9 billion, and DoorDash is only $123 million (the first full year profit in 2024). The difference is not several times that. The USS net interest rate for GAAP is over 10%, and DoorDash is just 1.1%, and the DoorDash 1.1% is still short of the $1.5 billion equity incentive before "profit" — and if SBC is included, the real profitability of DoorDash is still weak.

Uber Eats did not even disclose the GAAP profits separately, but only to the adjusted EBITDA, indicating whether the GAAP dimension was likely to be either in deficit or in marginal gain.

# The core dilemma of the American takeout platform

Why is DoorDash and Uber Eats so hard to make money? A few structural reasons:

- ** High unit cost but low frequency**: US-based flyer unit cost $30-40 (including distribution and tip), but users may only order one or two times a week. High unit cost means that users are sensitive to additional costs, and the platform has a small price increase space
- **The cost of riders is very high**: the hourly rate of the United States riders is much higher than China, and the cost of distribution is high, with a small fee culture
- ** Multi-platform competition**: US users are common in the simultaneous DoorDash and Uber Eats, with low loyalty, and the platform is forced to continue to subsidize and to offer membership preferences to keep users.
- **SBC (equity incentive) is an implicit cost**: EBITDA 1.9 billion after DoorDash has been adjusted looks good, but SBC spends ~1.5 billion - real free cash flow is limited

# Six, the revised overall judgment

The earlier view was that "the US Corps is like DoorDash, Uber Eats," and looking at the data, it needed to be corrected: the US Corps was in a much better position than its American counterparts.

- The Corps has proven that takeout can make money and that it's not low.
- DoorDash and Uber Eats are still on their way to prove their continued profitability.
- The size of the USS (10 times the order) gives a unit economic advantage of crushing.

The "profit ceiling" problem facing the US leg sale is true (higher costs for riders, regulatory pressure), but the starting point is far higher than that of the US counterpart. Even if profit margins are compressed by a few percentage points, the US leg is still the world’s most lucrative export platform, none.
