# In danger one: Social security of riders...

# A little money won't come

The United States has about 7 million registered riders, of whom 3.36 million are active annual riders. The vast majority of them are under the "blank" model – riders sign contracts with third-party companies or pick up their own statements in their personal capacity, and are not regular employees of the United States.

This is a common practice across the immediate distribution industry and is the cornerstone of the American Mission cost structure.

The question is: The policy direction is changing. Since 2025, several pilot riders have been insured for occupational injuries. Some cities have started to demand that platforms pay basic social security for riders. Hungry has already been piloting the minimum hourly pay in Shanghai, which is no less than $25.

If the "Five Risks One Full Cover" is really in place, the total cost would increase by $35-56 billion per year, estimated at an additional $10,000 to $17,000 per year per active rider (including the social security contribution). For reference, the adjusted net profit for the best year in the history of the United States (2024) would be $35.8 billion.

That is, the full landing cost of the Cavalry Society is approximately equal to the entire profits of the United States Regiment.

# But the odds are low

Several practical constraints:

First, a significant proportion of the 3.36 million riders are part-time or flexible jobs.

The goal of regulation is to "protect rights and interests" , not "kill jobs." A step-by-step push to raise user costs could lead to a reduction in platform use and cyclist unemployment – not the result that regulation wants to see.

Third, the policy course is "progressive move." Piloting occupational injury insurance (with an increase of about 0.3-0.5 dollars per unit) and gradually expanding coverage.

# And judge #

The cost of rider social security will continue to rise, and that's certain. The compliance cost of the new $50-10 billion scale per year is a approximate event. But the extreme scenario of the "a one-time hit of $35-56 billion" is not more than 10%.

The real risk is not that one step is in place, but that the cost is rigid, one-way up -- it's only getting higher and never going back. It's like a slow-tight rope that keeps compressing the American conservancy.

# In danger three: the ceiling for profit... gravity

# Takeout is a business of making hard money

The operating profit margin (OPM) for American take-out sales is about 3-4%. According to data for 2024, a single take-out group makes about a fortune.

It is not because the competition is too intense to push down profits, but the cost structure of the business determines that it is naturally cheap -- each with the cost of a rider, the cost of a platform, and the cost of a merchant subsidy. Even if the pattern of competition is completely stable, it is difficult to sell out OPM five to six percent.

The real profit contributor is the liquor store, where over 35% of the OPM is a typical light asset advertising + commission model. But the penetration of the shattering into the shop is a real threat. The twitching video-based grass + the group purchase write-off model, although only 1.5% of the take-out (the distribution network cannot be built), has developed a strong sense of ownership in the shop shopping.

Immediate retail (stock purchase + Elephant Supermarket) is considered to be the second growth curve, with GMV already exceeding 30 billion but still in a loss period. The business loss for the new business as a whole increased from 8.3 per cent to 9.7 per cent in 2025.

# The core question: Will the Corps always be a company with a "high income but very low profit"?

The US government collected $365 billion in 2025, a huge loss of $23.4 billion. Even if it returns to its 2024 profit position (adjusted net profit of $35.8 billion), the net profit rate is only about 10%.

The positive argument is that the platform’s profits are from commission and advertising, not distribution itself. Distribution is infrastructure, designed to lock traffic into the American family’s ecology, and then realize it through high-morigin businesses like shops, flashes, and advertising.

The counterarguments are that the costs of riders are rising in rigidity (see pitfall one), that competition subsidies are difficult to eliminate in the short term, and that the shop faces a vibrator. The ceiling of profits may be lower than the optimists think.

# And judge #

The American regiment probably does not become the 30% plus ROE profit machine with more than a 30% plus ROE. Its business model determines that it is a "rich-income, medium-profit" company. In the best case, the takeout of the OPM is restored to 3-4%, 35% + to the shop, immediate retail is going to the profit/loss balance, and the overall net profit rate is back to 8-12%.

This profit margin is not high in Internet companies, but it's reasonable in the business of "three meals a day for hundreds of millions of people." The key is whether it can be stabilized -- not re-established in a subsidy war every two years.
