# Why is it hard to make money on the American takeout platform

DoorDash and Uber Eats are hard to make money, not because they won't run.

The cost structure of selling out in the United States is heavier.

The original report referred to several core reasons.

First, the cost of the passenger unit is high but often low.

The US take-out bill often amounts to $30 to $40, with a higher cost of distribution and tips. Users pay “heavi” each time, and naturally it's not as high as Chinese.

Secondly, the rider is expensive.

The high labour costs in the United States also impose high compliance costs on the fee culture and regulatory requirements. The distribution costs are even higher for each single package of platforms.

Thirdly, multiplatform competition is equally serious.

Users are common for both DoorDash and Uber Eats.

Fourth, equity incentives are hidden costs.

The EBITDA after DoorDash has been adjusted looks good, but equity incentives dilute shareholders. For long-term investors, GAAP profits and free cash flows are more important.

So, the U.S. takeout platform is not without good companies.

Their problem is that a business that is costly to perform and that has less frequent users, is a high-profit platform that takes longer and requires a higher liquidity.

# DoorDash is expensive, why can't you just look at the sky

In turn, the high valuation of DoorDash was not necessarily an error.

The market is willing to value DoorDash high, and it's a few things that are in custody:

First, there is room for increased penetration of United States take-out sales.

Secondly, Dashpass members can improve user stickyness.

Third, advertising could become a high-morigin source of income.

Fourth, international operations such as Wolt and Delivero could be expanded by integrating them.

Fifth, scale effects eventually release higher profitability.

These logics are not without logic.

However, they are demanding high implementation and high valuation error rates.

When a company's price already contains many future successes, investors ask a simple question:

Even if the company does it right, how much can the shareholders make?

# What does this set of comparisons really mean?

The global takeout platform comparison is not intended to prove that “the United States must be better than DoorDash”.

It is truly clear that the market is different for different countries, for different narratives, for companies at different profit stages, and that the pricing is very different.

The problem with the United States Corps is that profits are interrupted by competition, but it has proven to be large-scale.

The problem with DoorDash is high valuation, but it also provides room for demonstrating profit margins.

The problem with Uber Eats is that the quality of the business is good, but the takeout is only part of the Uber as a whole and cannot be valued separately on the takeout logic.

# This conclusion

The Corps is not a perfect company.

It faces subsidy wars, shock-to-show shocks, rider costs, regulation and long-term technological changes.

But it is placed in a global export platform, which is another few that have proven to be truly profitable on a large scale.

This is the source of the valuation divergence.

The blanks are staring at the competition and losses in 2025;

The view is that the size and profit of 2024 are the most visible.

The market priced it between the two, but it clearly gave a very heavy discount.

For investors, it is not crucial to argue whether the American Order is the “most powerful export platform in the world”.

The key is to judge:

** 2024 profitability is the normal pattern of future returns or a high light before the price war**

There is no single answer to this question, and only the intensity of subsidies, changes in shares, rider costs and cash flows over the next few quarters will be used to validate this.
