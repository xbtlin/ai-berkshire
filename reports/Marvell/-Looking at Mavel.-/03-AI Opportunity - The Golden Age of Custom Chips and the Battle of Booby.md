# Customise ASIC logic: Why do cloud vendors not use all of it

Yvetteda GPU is so strong -- AWS still spends billions of dollars on Trainium, Microsoft on Maia, Google on TPU. ** This is not about the mood, it's about a clear economic account**.

Three core causes:

# Reason one: Economic accounts of the scenario

The two phases of AI — training and reasoning — require a very different kind of hardware.

The stage, the characteristics, the hardware requirements, the best fit.
|------|------|---------|---------|
<unk> Training Large-scale one-time calculation Flexibility > Efficiency GPU (Investigable)
<unk> Continuous high frequency, large scale > Flexibility <unk> ASIC (Cutting Chip) <unk>

Similarity: Training like "workshop" requires a single-power machine bed; reasoning like "waterline production" requires specialized equipment.

When AI's reasoning becomes the largest computing expense item for cloud producers,** the efficiency of each dollar per watt becomes extremely critical. Customizing ASIC performance energy consumption on a given reasoned task can be higher than the generic GPU ** 3-5 times ** – the larger the scale, the more money saved.

# Reason two: supply chain security

AWS has an annual income of over **100 billion**, and if AI's infrastructure is entirely dependent on British Weida, it is a risk that it will be a supplier. This is unacceptable in any industry.

Self-research + External Customised ASIC (Mail/Both) = Second Supply Source.

# Reason three: Differential competition

Google's TPU, AWS' Trainium, Microsoft Maia -- every cloud manufacturer wants to build a unique advantage on AI infrastructure. General GPU can be bought by everyone, but you are the only one who customise the chip.

** Reverse view:** The logic is established, but there is an implicit premise -- "Cutting ASIIC is a good enough price for it." British Weeder is not a static target. Blackwell architecture has greatly improved the efficiency of reasoning. If GPU's reasoning is kept up, the economic advantage of ASIC may be reduced to "not worth custom." Historically, it has been rare that generic programmes have eventually outperformed the exclusive programme (x86 versus various specialized processors).
