## How does Mastercard make money?

Many people's understanding of Mastercard remains as "a credit card company". This is the biggest misunderstanding.

**MasterCard does not issue cards, make loans, hold your deposits, or bear your bad debts. **

What it does is extremely simple: operate a global payments network. Every time a transaction is completed through this network - you swipe your card at Starbucks, place an order on Amazon, swipe your UnionPay/Mastercard dual-label card in Tokyo - Mastercard charges a small fee, usually a few basis points of the transaction amount.

The essence of this business is not banking or finance, but "toll booth"**.

Let’s compare it to:
- A highway toll station does not own the vehicles on the road, no matter what goods you transport - it only collects tolls
- Mastercard doesn't own your money, no matter what you buy - it only charges "tolls"

The difference is: MasterCard's "toll station" covers a payment network in more than 200 countries and regions around the world, with more than 100 million merchants and more than 3 billion cards in circulation.

## Light to the extreme business model

A typical card transaction involves four parties:

```
Cardholder (you) → Issuer (your bank) → MasterCard Network → Acquirer (merchant’s bank) → Merchant
```

In this chain:
- **Card Issuer** bears the credit risk (if you don't pay back, it's the bank's problem)
- **Acquiring bank** is responsible for connecting with merchants
- **MasterCard** is only responsible for the middle step - **authorization, clearing, and settlement**

It does not touch money, goods or risks. It only ensures that "transaction information is delivered within a few hundred milliseconds."

How light is this business model?

| Comparison Dimensions | Mastercard | Large US Bank (JPMorgan Chase) |
|---------|---------|---------------------|
| Number of employees | ~33,000 people | ~300,000 people |
| Credit risk exposure | **0** | Trillions of dollars |
| Capital intensity | Very low | Very high |
| Net profit margin | **45.7%** | ~30% |
| During a recession | Transaction volume falls, but no bad debts | Bad debts soar |

**Mastercard is essentially a technology company disguised as finance. ** Its core assets are not capital, but network, data and trust.

## It’s not a company, it’s an infrastructure

Here’s an easy-to-overlook fact: **MasterCard’s competitor isn’t other companies—it’s cash. **

A large amount of global annual consumer spending is still completed in the form of cash:
- Developed markets: Cash ratio is still **10-30%**
- Emerging markets (India, Southeast Asia, Latin America, Africa): cash accounts for more than **50%**

For every banknote replaced by an electronic payment, MasterCard charges an additional "toll".

This means that Mastercard's growth does not rely on "grabbing share from Visa", but on the decades-long long-term trend of "global payment migration from cash to digital".

**Consideration**: Some would say that government-driven real-time payment systems such as UPI in India and Pix in Brazil, with extremely low or even free rates, will bypass card networks. That's a valid concern - but these systems are currently largely limited to their home markets and have little competition in cross-border payments, Mastercard's highest-margin business. This will be discussed in detail in Chapter 04.

## A simple thinking framework

Looking at MasterCard, there is no need for complicated models. Just answer three questions:

**Question 1: Will the global trend from cash to digital payments be reversed? **
If the answer is "no" - Mastercard's pie is getting bigger.

**Question 2: In this trend, can MasterCard's "toll booth" status be bypassed? **
If the answer is "not in the short term" - Mastercard can keep charging.

**Question 3: Is the premium paid for this business at the current price (PE ~31 times) reasonable? **
This is where judgment really comes into play.
