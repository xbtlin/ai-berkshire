# Risk two: Stable Currency/Central Bank Digital Currency - Subversive or Integration?

This is the biggest structural concern of the current market**.

# The logic of threat theory

- Stabilizing currencies (e.g. USDC, USDT) theoretically achieves ** point-to-point payments**, bypassing the card network
- 2025 The total amount of stable currency transfers exceeded the total of Visa + Mastercard (although most of them were speculative transfers rather than consumption)
- Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) may not require intermediaries if it is operated directly by the central bank
- Monthly expenditure on stabilizing currency consumption cards increased from about $100 million** in early 2023 to about $1.5 billion** by the end of 2025**

Source: [CryptoNews Stability Currency Analysis] (CyptoNews Analysis)https://cryptonews.net/news/analytics/32583970/)

The logic of the MasterCard is:** Consumers don't care what the bottom technology is -- they care about "Crush cards work."** Whether the settlement is in dollars, euros or USDC, the consumer's experience with the card does not change. It's just the way the settlement ends.

And the value of the MasterCard in this chain — ** authentication, fraud detection, compliance review, global connectivity** — is required in whatever currency is used at the bottom.

# A combination of judgment #

The threat of currency stabilization to the MasterCard is more likely to be a long-term encroachment** than a short-term subversion**:

- ** Short term (1-3 years)**: negligible impact. Stable consumer payments are still early, with $1.5 billion spent on monthly consumer cards compared to annual GDR payments of $10.6 trillion, less than two-thousandths.
- ** Mid-term (3-7 years)**: may divert some cross-border remittances and B2B payments. But the MasterCard has been actively integrated through BVNK and MTN
- ** Long term (7 years or more)**: If stable currency/CBDC becomes the mainstream mode of payment, the MasterCard may need to be converted to "pay-per-contribution" instead of "card network". But its network effects, data assets and compliance capacity are still valuable

** The counter-view**: The optimists believe that the aggressive embrace of MasterCard has resolved the risk of currency stabilization. But many of the downtrodden companies in history – Koda, Nokia – have tried to embrace new technologies. The key is not "attitude," but ** whether new technologies have eliminated the demand for middlemen.** If some technology could be used to allow two people to transfer "freely and without friction" anywhere in the world, the "toll station" model of MasterCard would be a real challenge.

The high profit margins of MasterCard and Visa are essentially a "network usage tax" imposed on consumers and businesses around the world. Such high profitability naturally attracts regulatory attention.

# Potential regulatory risks

- **Afro-monopoly review**: The double-oligarchy pattern continues to be a cause for concern, but there is no substantive split motion at present
- ** Emerging market tariff pressure**: Governments may require lower rates to promote financial inclusion
- ** Data privacy**: Data realization operations (value-added services) on a MasterCard may face stricter privacy regulations

# The actual impact on profits

The exchange rate adjustment does reduce the income of the MasterCard. But there are two buffers:

** Exchange fees are only a portion of the GDR income** - value-added services have exceeded 40 per cent and are not subject to exchange fee regulation
** Downward adjustment of exchange fees * More acceptance of total payments by businesses * - lower rates may be offset by increased transaction volume

** The counter-view**: This "volume-price-slash" logic was established in the past, but if regulation continues to increase and tariffs are pressed to very low levels, the profit margin for the basic payment of the MasterCard may be significantly reduced. At that time, the overall profit margin will be sustained only by value-added services.

# This is the end of the story: what makes a "failure"?

To sum up with a back-up mentality — to make the business of MasterCard truly lethal, it is necessary to:

1. **The main global countries have simultaneously launched an interconnected retail payment system with zero rates** and very low probability
2. ** Some technology completely eliminated the need for "trust broker" in payments**
3. ** Global synchronisation significantly depressed exchange to near zero** may occur locally, but global synchronisation is unrealistic
4. ** Ongoing global deglobalization, with a significant contraction in cross-border transactions**

** Consolidated judgement**: The risk of a MasterCard is ** real but manageable**. No single risk can subvert its business model, and management has shown sufficient proactive and executive capacity to respond to it.

The real risk may not be a "business deviation", but ** "short-term reverse wind when the valuation is too high" ** -- that is the next question to be discussed.
