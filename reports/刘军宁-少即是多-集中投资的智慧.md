# Liu Junning: Less Is More - The Wisdom of Focused Investing

Source: Liu Junning, *The Philosophy of Investing: The Lamp of Conservative Wisdom*
Reprint: Sina Blog [original link](https://blog.sina.com.cn/s/blog_4462623d0102e2m7.html)

---

When it comes to the philosophy of value investing, there are two paradoxical but very true four-character sayings. One is "slow is fast," meaning that if you wait patiently, you often earn your return faster than if you rush. I discussed that in *The Place of Time in Value Investing*. The other is "less is more," meaning that investment quality matters more than investment quantity. That is the idea this article explores. "Less is more" answers the question of concentration versus diversification in investing.

The most accurate way to describe concentrated investing is probably focused investing, or disciplined investing: putting a limited amount of intelligence, energy, and capital into a small number of low-priced, high-quality assets can produce greater returns. The most authoritative early advocate of concentrated investing was Fisher. He was a foundational figure in conservative investing philosophy and the author of *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits*. He once said that investors are overtrained in the importance of diversification. Their fear of having too many eggs in one basket can lead them to buy too few companies they actually understand, and too many companies they do not understand at all. They never seem to ask whether buying stock in a company without fully understanding it may be even more dangerous than being insufficiently diversified. Fisher's philosophy was that less means more. Buffett and others later provided powerful real-world proof of that idea through their results.

Should you place your eggs in one basket and watch that basket carefully, or spread them across multiple baskets to avoid one loss taking down everything? In other words, should you concentrate or diversify? The answer is not really about how to concentrate or how to diversify, but about how to understand both concepts. For institutional investors, especially mutual funds, there are industry norms requiring a degree of diversification across market risk, industry risk, company risk, and even country risk. I will leave those rules aside. Here I am concerned only with concentration and diversification when there are no such constraints.

There are two often-overlooked facts. First, using securities investing as an example, most investors begin with diversification - buying many stocks with very little money. Even the greats who are now recognized as masters of stock investing started that way. Only some of them gradually moved from diversification to concentration. In that sense, concentration and diversification are often sequential rather than oppositional.

Second, professional investors also have their own private investments outside their institutional work - stocks, real estate, collectibles, and so on. So privately, they are usually retail investors in one form or another. If you organize those personal investments in aggregate, they are broadly diversified. No one puts all of their private wealth into one or two holdings. Once a person owns more than one property, or even one stock in a market different from their first, they are already diversified. If an investor holds one stock each in Shanghai, Shenzhen, A-shares, B-shares, the SME board, the ChiNext board, Hong Kong, and the U.S., they already own more than five stocks.

We can see that diversification is a natural tendency. If you do not deliberately concentrate, investing will usually become diversified by default. Diversification is natural; concentration must be learned. Too much diversification is the default state. Relative concentration is the goal to pursue.

Why concentrate? Because everyone is ignorant, and each person's circle of competence is limited. The essence of concentrated investing is not just the number of holdings, but focus. When asked what the most important key to his success was, Buffett answered "focus." "We only focus on a few of the finest companies. We are focused investors." The principle of concentrated investing is like a convex lens: it gathers limited sunlight and concentrates it onto a focal point to produce the desired temperature and effect. Without focus, you never reach that temperature.

A focused person can usually concentrate time, energy, and wisdom on the work at hand, maximizing initiative, creativity, and execution, and thereby achieving goals. Focus is not just for investors; it also applies to entrepreneurs and anyone trying to do something meaningful. The most successful entrepreneurs will concentrate their energy, talent, and resources on a single cause and achieve major success, just as Gates did with Microsoft, Jobs with Apple, and Dell with Dell Computer.

The feature of concentrated investing is to keep the good and gradually filter out the poor, leaving only a few exceptional assets. Diversified investing, by contrast, often gets linked to the strange habit of "taking profits" - or, more accurately, taking profits too early. Among a basket of stocks, diversified investors tend to sell the ones that have gone up first, say by 30%. If that mindset continues, the result is always to sell the winners and keep the losers, uproot the flowers and leave the weeds, until none of the stocks are doing well anymore.

A diversified investor may luckily buy a ten-bagger, but he may only own 100 shares, and may sell after a 30% gain. Treating securities is a bit like treating cards in a card game. Someone who does not know how to play throws away the good cards carelessly and keeps a bad hand. A good player tries to throw away the bad cards and keep a few strong ones for the key moments. Diversified investing often means the weak survive and the strong are eliminated. Concentrated investing means the strong survive and the weak are eliminated. In that sense, concentrated investing is the higher state.

The idea of concentration is closely tied to the concept of the circle of competence. Concentrated investing means concentrating within your circle of competence and only investing in what you understand. Safety comes from understanding the investment, not from the number of holdings. Diversification cannot make up for a lack of understanding. When your advisor tells you to diversify, he is also protecting himself, because he does not really know where your money should go either. Investors should protect themselves by improving their understanding of what they own, not by simply increasing the number of positions they hold.

Both the advocates of concentration and the advocates of diversification share one premise: nobody can predict or control the future. A hurricane, an earthquake, a new policy, a technological breakthrough, or a product recall can all cut the value of your assets in half. Diversifiers say that because the future cannot be predicted, you must diversify. Concentrators say that because the future cannot be predicted, you must concentrate. But diversification does not remove risk; it only swaps one set of risks for another.

Neither concentration nor diversification is good in its extreme form. The extreme of concentration is betting everything on one idea. If someone hears "concentrated investing" and, without thinking, puts all their money into a single thing they do not understand, that is speculation and gambling, not investing. The extreme of diversification is to scatter pepper over everything: spreading limited money over countless holdings. The result is that broad planting leads to a thin harvest, or even no harvest at all.

Too much diversification leads to too little return. That is why "more can be less." If instead you spread limited seeds across a few carefully chosen and cultivated plots, the harvest can be abundant. That is why "less can be more." Pepper-sprinkling is easy. Betting everything on one idea is also easy. Both can be done without thinking. If diversification is not pepper-sprinkling and not broad planting with thin results, and if concentration is not blind all-in betting, then concentration and diversification are no longer a false dilemma.
