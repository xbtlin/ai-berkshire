## A set of numbers that will let you re-understand Qualcomm

Approximately 1.2 billion smartphones are sold globally every year. The vast majority of flagship Android phones have a Snapdragon processor; regardless of whether the processor is from Qualcomm or not, mobile phone manufacturers almost have to pay Qualcomm another amount of money - patent licensing fees. This is Qualcomm's business: selling chips and collecting "tolls."

This understanding is not wrong, but it is far from enough. Let’s look at a set of numbers first:

## What exactly is Qualcomm?

The most common misunderstanding is to equate Qualcomm with "Snapdragon processor". Snapdragon is indeed Qualcomm's most well-known product, but Qualcomm's business model is much more complicated than "selling chips."

Qualcomm operates two distinct businesses at the same time:

### QCT: Chip technology (accounting for about 85% of revenue)

Design and sell mobile phone processors, baseband chips, RF front-ends, automotive chips, PC processors, and IoT chips. FY2025 revenue approximately **$37.7 billion**. This is an asset-heavy business that requires continuous huge investment in research and development and faces fierce competition.

### QTL: Technology licensing (accounting for about 15% of revenue, but contributes huge profits)

Based on the standard essential patents Qualcomm has accumulated in the 3G/4G/5G fields, it collects patent licensing fees from global mobile phone manufacturers. FY2025 revenue **$6.4 billion**, pre-tax profit **$4 billion**, profit margin over **63%**.

The nature of these two businesses is completely different:

| Dimension | QCT chip | QTL patent authorization |
|------|---------|------------|
| Nature | Sell products | Collect "tolls" |
| Margins | Moderate (squeezed by competition) | Very high (>63%) |
| Moat | Medium (requires continued R&D leadership) | Extremely deep (standard essential patents cannot be bypassed) |
| Growth space | Depends on new market development | Limited (tied to mobile phone shipments) |
| Analogy | More like Nvidia | More like a highway toll booth |

**The key to understanding Qualcomm is that these two businesses form a flywheel. ** Chip research and development continues to generate new patents and enrich the patent pool; the high profits from patent authorization provide stable funds for high-risk chip research and development. Cut off any piece and the other will be significantly damaged.

(Data source: Qualcomm FY2025 10-K)

## Three common misjudgments about Qualcomm

### Misjudgment 1: "Qualcomm sells mobile phone chips"

Mobile phone chips are indeed Qualcomm's largest source of revenue - in FY2026 Q2, QCT's mobile phone business quarterly revenue was approximately **6 billion US dollars**. But if you only see this piece, you'll miss several important changes:

- **Automotive chips**: FY2026 Q2 quarterly revenue is **1.33 billion US dollars** (+38% year-on-year), and the annualized rate has exceeded **5 billion US dollars**. The management target is to exit at an annualized rate of **6 billion US dollars** in FY2026 and reach **8 billion US dollars** in FY2029.
- **Internet of Things**: FY2026 Q2 quarterly revenue **$1.73 billion** (+9% year-on-year)
- **PC processor**: The Snapdragon X2 series occupies about **10%** of the Windows notebook market, and more than 100 design solutions have entered mass production or development stage
- **Patent Licensing**: Stable annual contribution of **4 billion USD+** pre-tax profit

The revenue share of non-mobile phone business has increased from about **30%** 3 years ago to about **40%**. Qualcomm is transforming from a "mobile phone chip company" to an "intelligent computing platform company" - the success or failure of this transformation will determine its story in the next five years.

(Data source: Qualcomm Q2 FY2026 financial report, PCWorld report)

### Misjudgment 2: "Apple develops its own baseband, Qualcomm is finished"

This is the most frequently mentioned bearish argument when investors discuss Qualcomm. The factual part is correct, but the conclusion needs to be calculated carefully.

**Fact**: Apple’s C1 self-developed baseband chip will be installed on iPhone 16e in 2025. C1 will make its way into more iPhone 17 models in fall 2026. The Ganymede baseband launched in 2026 will support millimeter waves. By 2027, Apple expects to completely discontinue Qualcomm baseband.

**Magnitude of impact**: Qualcomm’s annual baseband chip procurement revenue from Apple is approximately **5.7-5.9 billion** (Note: Different sources have different calibers. The US$2 billion used in the manuscript is a conservative estimate, and the latest data shows that the total amount including RF components is higher). This revenue will return to zero within 2-3 years.

**But there is a key "but"**: Apple's self-developed baseband does not affect patent licensing fees. For every iPhone sold, Apple still needs to pay patent licensing fees to Qualcomm's QTL department (about **$8** per unit), because 3G/4G/5G standard essential patents cannot be circumvented.

**Converse side of the argument**: Automotive chips are currently growing faster than Apple is losing revenue. Annual automotive revenue in FY2026 has exceeded US$5 billion, and the design win pipeline of US$45 billion means that this growth curve is far from peaking. Management is fully prepared for Apple's decoupling. This is not a "sudden stabbing" risk, but a "known and being hedged" risk.

(Data sources: AppleInsider, Qualcomm Q2 FY2026 earnings conference call, Futurum Research)

### Misjudgment 3: "Semiconductor companies are all cyclical stocks and are not worth holding for a long time."

This judgment holds true for most chip companies, but Qualcomm needs to be discounted. The reason lies in the patent licensing business with a profit margin of over 63%.

Look at the data for FY2023: That year, the global mobile phone market was destocked, and Qualcomm’s total revenue plummeted **19.2%**. However, QTL's patent licensing revenue only declined slightly - because patent fees are collected based on mobile phone shipments, even if the mobile phone cannot be sold, Qualcomm can collect money as long as it is still in production.

**QTL is Qualcomm's "countercyclical cushion." ** It allows Qualcomm to not fluctuate as much as pure chip companies (such as MediaTek) when the industry goes down.

But this cushion is not infinite. QTL's revenue growth has basically peaked (binding global mobile phone shipments to about 1.2 billion units/year), and the real growth engine must come from QCT's new market.

(Data source: Qualcomm FY2023-FY2025 annual report)

## To understand Qualcomm, you need to answer four questions

If you had to define Qualcomm in one sentence, the closest version might be:

> **Qualcomm = a wireless communication toll station (QTL) with a profit margin of 63% + a chip design machine (QCT) that competes in the four tracks of mobile phones, cars, PCs, and the Internet of Things at the same time + a transformation story that is losing its largest customer (Apple) but trying to fill the gap with new customers. **
