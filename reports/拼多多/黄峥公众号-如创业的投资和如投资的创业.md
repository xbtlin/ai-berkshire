Recently I have met some institutional investors, and I have gradually learned how institutional investing works. Sometimes the investor's perspective is indeed quite different from the entrepreneur's. I never raised money when I was an entrepreneur, and when I did secondary-market investing I did not think deeply about that difference either. On the one hand, I am gradually understanding the difference between the institutional investor perspective and the entrepreneur perspective, which is something new and unfamiliar to me. On the other hand, I am reflecting on my own approach when I used to invest in the secondary market, and it seems to have been consistent with entrepreneurship, with not that many differences. Looking back now, perhaps the investment education I received and the way I was taught to run businesses were relatively niche, but over time the process has probably been effective. So I want to record some of the investing ideas and entrepreneurship ideas I had before. Comparing them may be interesting.

## 1. Investing Like Entrepreneurship

I understand Buffett's point that buying stocks is buying part of the company, and that you should look for good businesses and good partners with a long-term holding mindset. That is actually very similar to entrepreneurship. On the one hand, you need to pay attention to the business model and choose the right kind of business model - not every model works. You need to spend a lot of time studying the details of the model. On the other hand, you need to choose good "partners." When investing, you should treat the founder and CEO as future partners and ask yourself whether you would be willing to work with them for a long time. If the founder or CEO has a character or culture that makes long-term cooperation uncomfortable, then it is best not to buy on day one. Entrepreneurship is the same: if you feel someone is not a good fit for the team in the long run, even if they can be useful for a while, hiring them often ends in regret. If that person is not just a teammate but a partner, then it is not only regret - it can be catastrophic.

Besides good businesses and good teams, investing also requires care about whether the price is good. That may look different from entrepreneurship, but if you think about it more deeply it is actually the same. Buffett has often said that Charlie Munger evolved his ape into a human, because Charlie Munger made Buffett realize he should pay a reasonable price for a good company instead of spending time picking up cigar butts - cheap companies with some residual value, but usually not very good businesses. This is even easier to see in entrepreneurship. If a business is extremely easy to do and does not require much sacrifice, it is often not a good business that will generate a lot of cash flow over the long run. Good decisions are often hard decisions and involve painful trade-offs. A good company should spend its energy solving the right hard problems, not running around picking up a pile of peanuts. Chasing peanuts usually means you will not even collect the peanuts. That is completely different from the mindset of accumulating small wins into a big win. Another price-related point in investing is whether the deal's downside is bearable overall. From the entrepreneurship angle this becomes very concrete: when starting a business, you need to ask whether it will kill you. Survival is the first priority. At the same time, you also need to assess from another angle whether you can win and whether you have enough strength to invest and compete.

## 2. Entrepreneurship Like Investing

There is an old saying: men fear choosing the wrong career, women fear marrying the wrong husband. Just as investing requires close attention to the business model, entrepreneurship itself often depends on industry and business-model selection for a large part of the outcome. That means you need to spend a lot of time figuring out what the right thing is, and then think about how to do it right. Moving forward slowly in the right direction is far better than sprinting in the wrong direction. Buffett says he has seen many ordinary people make a lot of money in finance, and he has also seen many very smart, excellent people struggle in bad industries. The same is true in entrepreneurship as in investing: choosing matters more than effort. Moving forward on the right road, even a bit slower, is like earning 20% annual compounded returns for 20 years in investing - far better than being up 100% one year and down 50% the next.

In one conversation with MBA students, Buffett and Bill Gates asked the audience a question: if you treated each of your classmates as a company, and you had to invest all your money in just one of them for 5%, whom would you choose? You would often not choose the smartest or the most capable person, but the most trustworthy one. That metaphor is especially appropriate for choosing partners in entrepreneurship. What we need is a trustworthy long-term partner, not someone who looks talented but might stab you in the back.

When Buffett talks about investments, he often mentions the concept of a business moat. If we treat every decision in entrepreneurship as an investment decision, then we need to distinguish which things we spend time and money on are assets and which are costs. Things that deepen the moat over time are usually assets. Things that become worse with time can be seen as costs. In asset purchases, mistakes are not usually fatal; at worst, you paid too much. In cost purchases, waste is much more harmful, and often has negative side effects. In entrepreneurship there is a special kind of asset: people who unite around a certain culture. There is also a special kind of cost: spending money to buy labor or skills. Distinguishing and transforming between the two is very interesting, and it is also one of the highest-ROI things you can do. If every cost could become a value-appreciating asset, then CFOs and investors would probably all be smiling in their sleep. But interestingly, when people invest, this kind of major and differentiating judgment is often handled too casually and given far too little weight.
