# Three, where's the security margin?

# First level: $63.5 billion net cash

The net cash on the books is $163.5 billion, or 31% of the market value. Translation: ** You buy the whole net, 30% will be back from the account. ** The remaining 70% is a business that makes 37.3 billion dollars a year.

# Second floor: 49.6 billion free cash flows

Free cash flows of 49.6 billion per year — and capital spending of just 1.1 billion — mean that even if the Internet were to begin to grow and no new games were to be developed today, it could still produce nearly $50 billion in cash each year.

More crucial is the ** distributional willingness**: buybacks exceed $2 billion, dividends $2/ADS, and the distribution rate of 60 per cent - management is giving cash back to shareholders.

# Third layer: the resilience of play business

Twenty-three years of Nevers to the West – a product whose viability is a very difficult asset to replicate – have been a record. Even if all new games fail, and overseas expansion is blocked, the income from easy games is unlikely to fall off the cliff in the next three to five years, depending on the existing mix of young products.

** Three layers of security stacked: net cash is 30% of the market value + annual free cash flows close to 50 billion + core products are extremely viable.** This makes the probability of losing money very low.

# Four, what's the "value trap"?

The security margin is not as high as it is impossible to lose. If two things happen simultaneously, the current price may not be the bottom:

# 1. Game growth is stagnant

If, in the next 2-3 years:
- Dreams are going down in real terms while the online stream is going down.
- The vagabond failed to maintain an annual income of over $300 million (brand authorization is not cost-effective)
- No new blast products.
- The overseas income ratio is still around 10%.

In this scenario, $92.1 billion of game revenue could be in a slow downward path. A company with declining incomes, even with high profitability and good cash flow – the market would not give it a reasonable valuation.

##2. China’s discounts continue to expand

If the US-China relationship continues to deteriorate and the Chinese-US system discounts increase from the current 30-40 per cent to 50-60 per cent — then even if the fundamentals remain unchanged, the stock price may fall further.

** The probability of two things going together is moderate, but if it does -- there may be 15-25% downside space.**

# Six, $113 worth it?

Not give the exact answer, but give a framework of judgement.

# Ask yourself five questions before buying

** First, how do you judge that the Internet-friendly game is growing?** If you think that Dreams can last another 5-10 years, that the Queens can be more than medium, that the tracky AI business can run out -- the 13-fold PE buys a great payoff space. If you think that the young products are ageing, that there are no replacements, that there are no overseas players, that 13-fold PE is just "reasonable pricing".

** Second, how do you see this guy?** 45.5% of the shareholding means buying the net is buying the net. If you trust his product judgement and capital allocation -- this is a block. If you worry about the risk of "one person is the boss" -- this is a reduction.

** Third, $163.5 billion in net cash?** If you calculate it by 1:1 (cash is cash), it's only about 10 times PE, which is very cheap. But if you have doubts about whether a Chinese company can actually return it to its shareholders, the value of the cash needs to be discounted. The current 60% interest ratio and the continuous buyback is giving back to the shareholders, but will it change in the future?

** Fourth, how long are you holding?** Can a man-dive need a period of 1-2 years to grow a man-made AI business, 3-5 years to get a medium discount, and a narrow time to collect. Many variables are not available if the holding period is less than 3 years.

** Fifth, is there a better choice for the same money?** *Telephone 18-20 times PE, more money for business, stronger overseas -- 30% expensive but more risk-dispersing. The choice of the Internet or the choice of the channel depends on your preference for "favourable but concentrated" and "precious but scattered".

# Seven, last

Back to the contrast at the beginning of the series: a firm with a 33 per cent net interest rate, a free cash flow of $49.6 billion and a net cash of $163.5 billion was priced at 13 times PE.

After five readings, the logic of this pricing became clear -- ** The market is not pricing "old profit" but "future uncertainty" and "China discount"**.

Uncertainty arises from the fact that 82 per cent of games are single dependent, 10 per cent of overseas low-capacity, whether the vagaries are young and whether one man is at risk of governance.

China’s discounts come from China’s US-China relations, regulatory policies, and systematic underestimation of China’s portfolio.

Thirteen times the price, which is a combination of these factors. If you think these risks are over-magnified -- that $113 is a price with a security margin. If you think these risks are real and they could worsen -- even 13 times the price does not constitute enough security margin.

** Both judgements make sense.** The difference is that you have different probabilities for each variable.

A company that makes a huge amount of money but faces challenges in growth, a founder of a gifted product but a conservative capital operation, a market that is underestimated by the geopolitical system — three things add up and constitute the subject of cyber-investment.

There is no standard answer to this question. But at least after reading these five, you should know what the question is.
