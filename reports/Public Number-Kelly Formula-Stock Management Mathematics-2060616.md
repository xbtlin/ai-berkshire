# Every pen makes money, but it's gone for a long time — the most counterintuitive mathematical trap in the investment

Assuming that there is an investment opportunity: 60% chance of winning, double the odds, and lose the price. That is, 20 bucks a math average for every 100 dollars -- a game that is obviously good for you.

What if you put all the money on it every time? Three wins, eight times the money -- but one loses, zero. And 40% of the probability of failure means that the more many wins, the more certain the fatal failure will come.

In 1998, the partners of long-term capital management companies included two Nobel laureates in economics, whose strategies did make money – but, with 25-fold leverage, a Russian debt crisis had cost US$ 4.6 billion to die. Their mistakes were not wrong, but too heavy.

The question is not "should or shouldn't?" It's "How much." Sixty years ago, an informationist gave a precise answer.

# Kelly formula: how much do you want to bet?

In 1956, the physicist John Kelly of Bell's lab resolved the question: What proportion of money should be invested at each time in a game known for the odds and odds in order to make long-term wealth grow the fastest?

The answer is in a big white:** the winnings minus the losing ones, divided by the odds, are the proportion you should be in.**

The formula is:

> Best warehouse = (Creation x Win - Fail) <unk>

The letter is f = (b×p-q) / b, where f is the best rate of bet, b is the odds (how many times the winnings), p is the odds, q is the odds of being lost (1-p).

Back to the beginning: double the winnings (b=1), 60% (p=0.6), 40% (q=0.4), in place: f = (1 x 0.6 - 0.4) / 1 = 20%. The Kelley formula tells you that 20% of total funds are the fastest growing strategy in the long run.

For another one: double the winning rate (b=2) and 50%. f = (2 x 0.5 - 0.5) / 2 = 25%. The better the odds, the more.

Less, slower growth. More. Not faster growth -- slower growth, even backwards. That's what Kelly's formula is all about.

# Why is the loss 50% worse than you think

The fatality of excessive bets stems from the mathematical asymmetries of losses.

[loss asymmetric]../assets/kelly/fig2-loss-asymmetry.png)

How much more is needed to get back?
|---------|-------------|
| -10% | +11% |
| -20% | +25% |
| -30% | +43% |
| -50% | **+100%** |
| -75% | **+300%** |
| -90% | **+900%** |

It looks like a similar 11% increase, down by 10%. But it takes 50% to double. It takes 75% to triple. It takes 90% -- nine times.

The reason is simple: investment is not an addition. The 50% is multiplied by 0.5, and the 50% increase by 1.5, 0.5 x 1.5 = 0.75, is not equal to 1. Losses reduce the principal, rising from a smaller base to the same absolute value, and require a larger percentage.

** This is the mathematical reason for the Kelley formula: it naturally avoids a major reversal.** The Kelley formula maximizes the arithmetic growth rate (geometric average), not the arithmetic average. The natural penalty for logarithmic growth rates fluctuates significantly – because in the world of multiplication, a large loss offsets many small wins.

So the Kelly formula is not a formula for "making you more," but a formula for "helping you no less." Both things are the same thing in the long term compound.

# Masters fight: you see, you can't bet

If the Kelly formula is just a mathematical game, it won't interest investors. But the barfiette, Manger, and the eternal war-fighting operation, almost all of the living material of the Kelly formula, though they may never have used it.

Look first at an investment version of the Kelly formula. When the return is not a simple "win/lost" but a continuous distribution, this is the case with equities, the Kelly formula is changed to:

> Best warehouse = (expected rate of return - no risk rate) <unk> Square of fluctuations

The letter is the f = (expected gain - no risk rate) / variance. Big white: ** The higher the excess gain, the less volatility, the heavier the position. **

And there is an info-wise interpretation of this formula: your optimal growth rate is equal to your information advantage over the market. If you understand a company no deeper than the market, the best position is zero.

# Half Kelly: The real strategy to use in real battle

The whole of Kelly is the fastest growing in theory. But almost all serious practitioners recommend only half.

** First, your parameters must be wrong.** In casinos, the odds are accurate. In stock markets, the expected returns and fluctuations are guessed. If you overestimate your advantages, Kelly is super-Keli -- and we know what happens to Kelly. Half Kelly left the buffer zone for the error.

** Second, the loss on growth rates is much less than the decrease in volatility.** Half-Kelley sacrificed only 25% of growth rates, but reduced the volatility by 50%.

** Third, psychologically limited.** All Kelly may withdraw more than 50% in extreme cases. Half Kelly keeps the retreat within the limits of human psychology.

Research by mathematicians Rotando and Sopo confirms that:** half Kelly has a 90 per cent probability of avoiding a devastating loss, compared to 50 per cent for the whole of Kelly.**

Speaking of Sopo – he may be the greatest practitioner of the Kelly formula. The MIT math professor published Beating the Dealer in 1962, using the Kelly formula to guide him to blacklists at 21 points.

Sopo himself recommends half Kelly. The best of theory and reality is the best of arguments, the black swan event, and the mental limits of the human person.

# The best money strategy is to make money

The question goes back to the beginning: Why is every pen a positive expectation and a long-term possibility of bankruptcy?

Because investment is not a single game, it is an infinite game of repetition. In a repeat game, the final result is not arithmetic average (expected value), but a geometric average (complex growth rate). The Kelley formula maximizes exactly geometric averages.

In practical terms, three principles:

** First, do not place any bets without the information advantage.** Kelly's formula clearly states that when you understand a company more than the market consensus, the best position is zero. This is exactly the same as the "no touch" part of Yongping.

** Second, if there's an advantage, it's only half a Kelly. ** Your estimate of your advantage is almost certainly high. Use half the storage space, leave enough room for mistakes.

** Third, never exceed the cap of Kelly.** This is an inexorable red line. Over two times as much as Kelly, no matter how accurate the analysis, the long-term result is zero. LTCM and Archegos lost not their judgment, but their position.

Buffett says the first rule of investment is "Don't lose money," and the second rule is "Don't forget the first." These two rules sound like crap, but Kelly's formula proves by mathematically that it is the most profound investment wisdom.

> The investment is not the best of the people, but who can stay on the table. Living is more important than anything.
